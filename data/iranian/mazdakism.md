# Mazdakism

## Historical context

Mazdak appears in sources as a religious reformer active around the reign of the Sasanian king Kavad I. Later Zoroastrian, Arabic, and Persian authors portray his followers as social and cosmological radicals, but their accounts were written by opponents and often disagree. No securely identified Mazdakite scripture survives, and the relationship between Mazdak, an earlier teacher named Zardusht Khurragan, and later movements remains debated.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Reports associate the movement with nonviolence, restraint, communal access to resources, and a cosmology of light and darkness. Sensational claims about “sharing women” likely reflect polemic more than neutral description. Scholars variously connect Mazdakite ideas with Zoroastrian reform, Manichaean-like dualism, or Iranian social protest. The graph therefore treats its Zoroastrian branch as a minority reconstruction rather than settled institutional genealogy.
