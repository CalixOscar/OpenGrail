# Zoroastrianism

## Historical genesis

Zoroastrian tradition attributes its central revelation to Zarathustra (Greek: Zoroaster). His date and homeland remain disputed; linguistic comparison places the oldest hymns, the Gāthās, within an ancient Iranian setting often dated broadly between the late second and early first millennia BCE. Those hymns address Ahura Mazda and dramatize a moral choice between truth or right order (*aša*) and the lie (*druj*).

The surviving Avesta contains materials from different periods. Much was transmitted orally, and later Middle Persian works preserve interpretation as well as cosmology after the disruptions of conquest. Zoroastrian institutions were important under the Sasanian Empire, but it is unsafe to project the better-documented Sasanian system unchanged back onto the earlier Achaemenid court. Communities continue today especially in Iran, India, and a global diaspora.

## Ideas and practice

Zoroastrian ethics emphasizes good thoughts, good words, and good deeds. Fire is honored as a visible focus of purity and truth, not worshiped as an independent god. Later texts describe creation's struggle against destructive spirit, individual judgment, resurrection, and a final renovation (*frašō.kərəti*) in which evil is overcome. Scholarly debate continues over how the relation between Ahura Mazda and the hostile spirit should be characterized across textual layers.

## Comparative notes

Close linguistic and ritual correspondences connect early Iranian and Vedic materials to a shared Indo-Iranian inheritance. Possible Zoroastrian contributions to Jewish, Christian, and Islamic angelology, demonology, resurrection, and eschatology are important but difficult to isolate. Imperial contact supplies plausible routes, while late dates for many written sources complicate arguments about direction.
