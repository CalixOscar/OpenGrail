# Buddhism

## Historical genesis

Buddhist traditions trace their origin to Siddhārtha Gautama, the Buddha, who taught in northern South Asia sometime around the fifth century BCE. Precise dates remain debated. His community belonged to a wider *śramaṇa* landscape of wandering teachers who questioned sacrifice, status, selfhood, and the mechanisms of rebirth. Early teachings were preserved orally and organized in monastic collections before being written down centuries later.

Royal and mercantile patronage supported monasteries and missions across Asia. Distinct schools developed their own disciplinary and doctrinal canons. Mahāyāna movements elevated the bodhisattva path and circulated new sūtras; tantric forms later joined ritual, visualization, mantra, and initiation. Buddhism became deeply local in Sri Lanka, Central Asia, China, Korea, Japan, Tibet, Southeast Asia, and the modern global diaspora.

## Ideas and practice

The Four Noble Truths diagnose *dukkha*, trace it to craving and ignorance, affirm cessation, and prescribe a path of ethical conduct, meditation, and wisdom. Impermanence and dependent arising undermine the search for a permanent independent self. These formulations do not imply nihilism: Buddhist traditions analyze persons as causal continuities and cultivate compassion toward sentient beings. Monastic discipline, merit-making, ritual protection, pilgrimage, devotion, textual study, and meditation coexist in lived practice.

## Comparative notes

Buddhism arose in a world shaped partly by Vedic and Brahmanical institutions, yet “divergence” should not be mistaken for a one-time schism from a unitary Hinduism. Early Buddhists rejected Vedic sacrificial authority and reformulated shared terms such as karma and liberation. Later Buddhist and Hindu thinkers debated intensely while exchanging philosophical arguments, deities, artistic forms, and tantric methods. Transmission into East Asia generated further interaction with Daoist and Confucian vocabularies rather than simple substitution.
