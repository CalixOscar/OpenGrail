# Hindu Traditions

## Historical genesis

“Hinduism” is a relatively recent umbrella label for traditions with much older and varied histories. Between the late Vedic period and the early centuries CE, sacrificial lineages, renunciant movements, local cults, epic narratives, and new forms of devotion interacted across South Asia. Purāṇic worship of Viṣṇu, Śiva, the Goddess, and other deities grew alongside temple networks and domestic rites. No single founder, council, or creed defines the whole.

Vedic texts retained exceptional authority in many schools, but continuity did not mean simple preservation. Philosophers reinterpreted sacrifice, self, action, liberation, language, and ultimate reality. Devotional communities composed vernacular poetry and established regional canons. Tantric traditions developed ritual technologies shared in different forms by Śaiva, Śākta, Vaiṣṇava, Buddhist, and Jain practitioners.

## Ideas and practice

Frequently recurring concepts include *dharma* (order, duty, teaching), *karma* (action and consequence), *saṃsāra* (repeated birth), and *mokṣa* (release). Their meanings differ by genre and school. Vedānta debates whether self and ultimate reality are nondual, qualified, or distinct; Sāṃkhya analyzes consciousness and material nature; devotional traditions foreground grace and relationship. Pilgrimage, image worship, festivals, recitation, yoga, sacrifice, and household observance all belong to Hindu histories.

## Comparative notes

The graph's **diverged from** relation to Vedic religion is a visualization shorthand for transformation and expansion, not replacement. Hindu and Buddhist communities argued with one another, shared institutions and vocabulary, patronized neighboring sites, and sometimes adopted related ritual forms over more than a millennium. Their connection is consequently modeled as reciprocal cultural synthesis rather than a claim that either tradition is merely a derivative of the other.
