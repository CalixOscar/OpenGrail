# Brahma Kumaris

## Historical context

The Brahma Kumaris ("Daughters of Brahma") was founded in 1937 in Hyderabad, Sindh (then in British India, now Pakistan) by Dada Lekhraj Kripalani (1876–1969), a successful Sindhi diamond merchant. In the mid-1930s, Lekhraj experienced a series of powerful visionary revelations depicting the cosmic dissolution of the present world through natural cataclysms and nuclear fire, followed by the dawn of a paradisiacal Golden Age. In response, he liquidated his business and formed a devotional spiritual community originally known as *Om Mandali*.

In a radical departure from traditional Hindu patriarchal structures of the period, Lekhraj transferred all his property and organizational authority to a managing trust composed entirely of young women, headed by Radhe Pokardas Rajwani (revered as Mamma or Jagadamba Saraswati). This structure provoked fierce opposition from the local Hindu merchant community (organized as the Anti-Om Mandali Committee), who fiercely objected to married and unmarried women asserting spiritual autonomy and adopting strict vows of celibacy. Following the 1947 Partition of India, the community relocated in 1950 to Mount Abu in Rajasthan, India, which remains its global spiritual headquarters (*Madhuban*). In the post-war era, the movement expanded worldwide as the Brahma Kumaris World Spiritual University (BKWSU), establishing thousands of centers across more than 110 countries and gaining consultative NGO status with the United Nations.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

A distinguishing feature of the Brahma Kumaris is its complete administrative and spiritual governance by women (*Dadis* and *Didis*), who serve as senior teachers, regional coordinators, and spiritual guides. 

The movement's core theological claim posits a closed, identical 5,000-year cosmic cycle divided into four equal ages of 1,250 years each: the Golden Age (*Satya Yuga*), Silver Age (*Treta Yuga*), Copper Age (*Dvapara Yuga*), and Iron Age (*Kali Yuga*), culminating in a brief transitional Confluence Age (*Sangam Yuga*). During this Confluence Age, God—understood as the incorporeal, genderless Supreme Soul (*Śiva Bābā*)—channels divine knowledge through chosen human mediums, instructing souls in moral purification.

Spiritual practice centers on open-eyed *Rāja Yoga* meditation, wherein students sit in silent contemplation facing a teacher or a gently illuminated red oval symbolizing the Supreme Soul. Practitioners visualize themselves as detached points of light animating the physical body and cultivate pure thoughts to dissolve past karmic impressions (*saṃskāras*). Members adhere to rigorous daily disciplines, including total celibacy, vegetarianism, abstinence from alcohol, tobacco, and gambling, and attendance at daily early-morning discourses (*Murlis*).
