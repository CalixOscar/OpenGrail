# Humanistic Buddhism

## Historical context

Twentieth-century reformer Taixu promoted a “Buddhism for human life,” and Yin Shun further developed the language of Buddhism for the human realm. Later organizations in Taiwan and the Chinese diaspora—including Fo Guang Shan, Dharma Drum Mountain, and Tzu Chi—translated related ideals into education, charity, media, cultural work, and lay participation. These institutions remain organizationally independent and interpret the current differently.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Humanistic Buddhism locates the bodhisattva path in ordinary society rather than treating Buddhism chiefly as funerary ritual or escape from the world. It values ethical action, accessible teaching, community service, and the possibility of creating a Pure Land through transformed relationships. Its modern institutions use management and technology, but the movement grounds this adaptation in Mahāyāna precedents rather than presenting secular humanitarianism as a replacement for awakening.
