# Sōtō Zen

## Historical context

Dōgen returned from China in 1227 and established a Japanese lineage grounded in Caodong teaching; Eiheiji became an enduring center. Keizan Jōkin and later disciples expanded the network through rural temples, funerary service, devotion, and flexible transmission. Modern reforms systematized the school’s administration and promoted Dōgen’s writings in Japan and internationally.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Sōtō discourse gives special weight to *shikantaza*, or “just sitting,” and to Dōgen’s claim that practice and realization are inseparable. Daily liturgy, precepts, work, memorial rites, and teacher-student transmission are equally integral to institutional life. The familiar contrast between contemplative Sōtō and kōan-centered Rinzai is incomplete: historical Sōtō teachers also used kōan literature, and most parish temples serve broad ritual roles.
