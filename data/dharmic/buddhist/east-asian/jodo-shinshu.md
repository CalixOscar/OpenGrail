# Jōdo Shinshū

## Historical context

Shinran, a disciple of Hōnen, interpreted Pure Land teaching around radical dependence on Amitābha’s vow and did not initially establish a formal school. Communities organized around his descendants and writings, eventually developing powerful Honganji institutions and several branches. The tradition later spread through Japanese migration, forming durable communities in the Americas and elsewhere.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Jōdo Shinshū treats *shinjin*, the entrusting heart-mind given through other-power, as decisive; *nembutsu* expresses gratitude rather than accumulating merit to compel salvation. Its clergy may marry and its temples often function through family and lay participation, challenging simple monastic-versus-lay categories. “Faith Buddhism” can be a helpful comparison, but Christian analogies should not replace its Mahāyāna account of non-self, vow, and awakening.
