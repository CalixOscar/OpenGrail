# Shingon Buddhism

## Historical context

Kūkai returned from Tang China in 806 with esoteric initiations, texts, and ritual technologies that became Shingon. Mount Kōya and Tōji developed as major centers, while Shingon lineages served court, monastery, and local communities. The tradition adapted Indian and Chinese materials within Japanese institutional settings.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Shingon presents Mahāvairocana Buddha as the cosmic expression of reality and uses the “three mysteries” of body, speech, and mind to realize buddhahood in this very body. Mandalas organize ritual worlds, but practice requires graded initiation. Kami were historically interpreted through Buddhist frameworks and worshiped in shared sites; this relationship changed under nineteenth-century separation policies.
