# Animist Frameworks

## Historical context

Edward Tylor popularized “animism” in nineteenth-century anthropology as a supposed elementary stage of religion. That evolutionary hierarchy is rejected. Later scholars and Indigenous thinkers have selectively reworked the term to describe relational ontologies in which animals, places, weather, ancestors, or artifacts may act as persons within particular moral worlds.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Animism is not one global religion, creed, or developmental stage. It can be a comparative lens only when grounded in community-specific languages, sovereignties, and practices. Similar relations with land or nonhuman beings do not make Ainu, Yorùbá, Amazonian, Siberian, and Australian traditions interchangeable. This umbrella node exists to connect a concept while warning against flattening difference.
