# Rizalista Movements

## Historical context

The Rizalista movements emerged in the late 19th and early 20th centuries across Central and Southern Luzon following the execution of the reformist novelist and polymath Dr. José Rizal by the Spanish colonial government on 30 December 1896. Rizal's martyrdom crystallized anti-colonial sentiment and coincided with the Philippine Revolution and the Philippine-American War. In peasant consciousness, his death was interpreted through the familiar narrative framework of the *Pasyon* (the vernacular epic recounting the Passion and crucifixion of Christ), transforming Rizal into a redemptive messiah for the Filipino nation.

These movements drew deeply upon earlier 19th-century folk-Catholic brotherhoods (*cofradías*), notably Apolinario de la Cruz's (Hermano Pule) Cofradía de San José on Mount Banahaw in the 1840s. Over the early decades of the 20th century, numerous distinct Rizalista organizations formed, including the *Samahan ng Tatlong Persona Solo Dios* (founded by Agapito Illustre / Agapito Makabagdal in the 1910s), *Iglesia Watawat ng Lahi* (established in Lecheria, Calamba in 1936), and *Ciudad Mística de Dios* (founded in 1952 by Maria Bernarda Balitaan, rooted in earlier Banahaw visionary traditions).

Crucially, Dr. José Rizal was an Enlightenment-educated, liberal-democratic intellectual whose historical life and 1896 execution are thoroughly established matters of academic consensus. Rizal himself made no claims to divinity or messianic authority. His veneration as a divine incarnation, the second coming of Christ, or an immortal spiritual guide residing in Mount Banahaw represents the theological claims of his devotees and the creative syncretism of Philippine popular religion.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

A defining characteristic of the Rizalista traditions is their sacred geography centered on Mount Banahaw, an active volcano spanning Laguna and Quezon provinces regarded as the *Banal na Bundok* (Holy Mountain). Devotees understand Banahaw as the new Jerusalem, where natural rock formations, deep caves, rivers, and waterfalls serve as sacred pilgrimage stations (*puwesto*) named after biblical locations (such as Calvary, the Garden of Gethsemane, and the Jordan River). Pilgrims perform penitential treks across these stations to attain moral cleansing and spiritual power (*bisa* or *anting-anting*).

Rizalista groups frequently blend orthodox Catholic liturgy, Tridentine Latin prayer fragments, and Marian devotion with Philippine patriotic symbols, displaying the national flag beside altars of Christ and Rizal. Furthermore, several major Rizalista sects, most prominently Ciudad Mística de Dios, feature female spiritual leaders (*Suprema* and *priestesses*), reflecting a cultural continuity with pre-colonial *babaylan* shamanic roles preserved under Christianized symbolic forms.
