# Babaylanism & Philippine Folk Shamanic Revival

## Historical context

In pre-colonial Philippine societies prior to the Spanish arrival in 1521, spiritual leadership, community healing, and ritual intercession were primarily exercised by figures known in the Visayas as *babaylan* (also *baylan* in Mindanao and *katalonan* in Tagalog regions). These ritual specialists were predominantly women or feminized men (*asog* or *bayog*) who held esteemed positions as custodians of oral epics, herbalists, agricultural diviners, and intermediaries between the human community and ancestral or nature spirits (*anito* and *diwata*).

Following Spanish Catholic colonization, the babaylan were systematically targeted for suppression. Spanish missionary chronicles—such as the accounts of Pedro Chirino, Juan de Plasencia, Francisco Ignacio Alcina, and the Boxer Codex—demonized babaylan practices as witchcraft (*brujería*) and devil-worship. Shrines were burned, sacred wooden figures (*likha* or *taotao*) destroyed, and practitioners executed or driven into remote highland sanctuaries. Over centuries of colonial rule, elements of babaylan practice survived underground by syncretizing with folk Catholic devotions, giving rise to local healing traditions (*albularyo*, *hilot*) and messianic peasant brotherhoods.

During the late 20th and early 21st centuries, Babaylanism experienced a major intellectual and spiritual resurgence. Propelled by Philippine indigenous psychology (*Sikolohiyang Pilipino*), feminist theology, decolonial historiography (*Pantayong Pananaw*), and Filipino diaspora movements (such as the Center for Babaylan Studies in North America), contemporary scholars, artists, and community organizers have actively revived and reinterpreted babaylan principles as an egalitarian, decolonizing framework for cultural reclamation and holistic healing.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Contemporary Babaylanism must be understood as a modern reconstruction and cultural reclamation movement rather than an unbroken, direct institutional lineage. While the historical reality and prominent status of pre-colonial female and *asog* shamans are matters of academic consensus, claims of seamless, unadulterated esoteric transmission across four centuries of colonial hegemony are minority scholarly interpretations or devotional assertions. Much of the surviving documentary evidence for early practices remains filtered through hostile Spanish ecclesiastical sources, which must be carefully disentangled from indigenous oral traditions like the Panay *Hinilawod* and highland *Sugidanon* epics.

Unlike institutionalized world religions with rigid ecclesiastical hierarchies and fixed dogmatic creeds, modern Babaylanism emphasizes relationality, somatic awareness, and experiential spirituality. Central to this ethos are the indigenous concepts of *loob* (the relational inner self) and *ginhawa* (breath, life energy, and well-being). The movement shares broad comparative affinities with global neo-shamanic, indigenous revivalist, and eco-feminist movements, offering a non-dogmatic, nature-centered counterpoint to the patriarchal structures of Spanish-era Catholicism in the Philippines.
