# Lapiang Malaya

## Historical context

Lapiang Malaya ("Freedom Party") was founded in 1949 by Valentin "Tatay Ente" de los Santos, a charismatic orator from Rizal province. Emerging during the turbulent post-World War II period marked by agrarian discontent, the Hukbalahap rebellion, and widespread rural poverty, the movement bridged modern electoral politics and traditional Philippine millenarianism. De los Santos registered Lapiang Malaya as a political party and ran in multiple national elections, including presidential campaigns against Ramon Magsaysay and Carlos P. Garcia, mobilizing thousands of impoverished tenant farmers, urban laborers, and marginalized rural workers across Luzon.

The movement drew heavily upon the cultural repertoire of 19th- and early 20th-century peasant brotherhoods (*kapatiran*), such as the *Colorums*, the *Guardia de Honor*, and the *Sakdalistas*. Members viewed De los Santos as a prophetic figure endowed with divine wisdom and access to esoteric spiritual power. Lapiang Malaya campaigned on a platform of radical land reform, the abolition of unjust taxes, complete national sovereignty, and the moral renewal of Philippine society through Christian devotion and patriotic sacrifice.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Lapiang Malaya stood out for its synthesis of modern political organization with folk-Catholic mysticism and indigenous martial traditions. Members attended regular assemblies where they received spiritual instruction, recited Latinized prayers (*orasyon*), and prepared talismanic amulets (*agimat* or *anting-anting*). In public processions and political rallies, adherents wore striking ritual uniforms—blue or yellow tunics adorned with embroidered religious and patriotic emblems, red capes, and sashes—and carried ceremonial bolos (machetes).

Central to the movement's self-understanding was the belief that moral righteousness (*katwiran*) and faithful devotion conferred divine protection against physical harm, including firearm bullets. This conviction of invulnerability was a recurring feature of historical Philippine anti-colonial uprisings (such as the 1896 Katipunan and the 1930s Colorum revolts), reflecting a cosmological view wherein spiritual purity directly shielded the physical body.

## Documented harms and controversies

The history of Lapiang Malaya concluded in a catastrophic act of state violence in which the movement was the victim.

On 21 May 1967, approximately 400 to 500 members of Lapiang Malaya gathered along Taft Avenue in Pasay City, near Manila, intending to march toward Malacañang Palace to demand the resignation of President Ferdinand Marcos and the implementation of radical social reforms. The marchers were clad in their ritual capes and uniforms, armed primarily with ceremonial bolos and wooden staves, and carried their consecrated *anting-anting* in the sincere belief that God would shield them from harm.

Units of the Philippine Constabulary (PC) Metropolitan Command intercepted the marchers on Taft Avenue. When the crowd refused orders to disperse and continued to advance, state security forces opened fire with automatic rifles and shotguns. The shooting resulted in the deaths of approximately 33 Lapiang Malaya members on the street, with more than 40 others sustaining severe gunshot wounds.

Following the massacre, hundreds of surviving members were arrested and detained. Valentin de los Santos was spared trial on political charges by being declared legally insane by state authorities. He was forcibly committed to the National Mental Hospital (now the National Center for Mental Health) in Mandaluyong, where in 1969 he was beaten to death by a fellow patient under highly suspect circumstances that were never independently investigated. With its leadership killed and followers dispersed, the movement dissolved.
