# Sumarah

## Historical context

Sumarah (from the Javanese word meaning "total surrender" or "yielding") emerged in Yogyakarta, Central Java, in late 1935 through a series of mystical revelations (*wahyu*) received by Raden Ngabei Sukokinohartono (known affectionately as Pak Suko, 1897–1971), a Javanese court official and clerk. Pak Suko reported being guided by an inner voice to abandon traditional, arduous ascetic practices (*tapa*) in favor of an effortless, direct surrender to God Almighty (*Tuhan Yang Maha Esa*). Alongside co-founders Dr. Surono and Pak Hardjosapurno, he established *Paguyuban Sumarah* as an open fellowship dedicated to spiritual refinement.

The movement expanded during the late Dutch colonial era, the Japanese occupation, and the Indonesian National Revolution. In the post-independence era, Sumarah became one of the most prominent organizations representing *aliran kebatinan* or *aliran kepercayaan* (indigenous Javanese faith traditions). Along with other Javanese spiritual movements, Sumarah engaged in a prolonged struggle for state recognition within Indonesia's religious administration. While the Indonesian state historically required citizens to identify with one of six officially recognized world religions, a landmark 2017 Constitutional Court ruling affirmed the legal rights of *penghayat kepercayaan* (adherents of indigenous faiths) to record their faith on national identity cards, though full societal and bureaucratic equality remains an ongoing legal and social discussion.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Sumarah stands apart from older forms of Javanese *Kejawen* mysticism by explicitly rejecting severe physical mortification (such as prolonged fasting in rivers, sleep deprivation, or graveyard vigils) and occult magical pursuits (*ngelmu gaib*). Instead, it teaches that spiritual realization is accessible to all people through ordinary daily life by mastering the art of *sujud*—an internal posture of utter yielding and alignment with divine will.

Central to Sumarah's epistemology is the progressive cultivation of *rasa* (intuitive feeling, taste, or direct perception). Practice distinguishes between three levels of consciousness: *rasa jaba* (outer sensory perceptions), *rasa angen-angen* (mental imagination, thoughts, and emotional attachments), and *rasa sejati* (the pure, underlying divine intuition). In meditation sessions guided by experienced elders (*pamong*), practitioners sit quietly in chairs or on mats, progressively releasing physical tension and mental chatter until the mind settles into *hening* (profound stillness and clarity), allowing divine guidance to direct the individual's thoughts, speech, and actions.
