# Coconut Religion (Đạo Dừa)

## Historical context

The Coconut Religion (Đạo Dừa) was established in 1963 by Nguyễn Thành Nam (1909–1990), a French-trained chemical engineer from an aristocratic family in Bến Tre Province in the Mekong Delta. After returning from his studies in France in 1935, Nam abandoned his engineering career to pursue rigorous Buddhist and Daoist asceticism, meditating for three years on a stone slab at That Son (Seven Mountains) under a strict vow of silence and subsisting solely on coconuts and coconut milk.

In 1963, Nam moved to Cồn Phụng (Phoenix Island) on the Tiền River in Bến Tre, where he established a floating religious commune called the "Peaceful Realm" (*Tịnh Độ Cư Sĩ*). The commune grew into a sanctuary attracting thousands of war-weary followers, draft evaders, and international visitors. Nam made theatrical political interventions, running for the presidency of South Vietnam in 1971 on an absolute pacifist platform and offering to negotiate directly with North Vietnamese and American leaders. Following the communist reunification of Vietnam in 1975, the government outlawed the movement, dismantled the sanctuary, and placed Nam under state surveillance until his death in 1990, after which the organized community dissolved.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Đạo Dừa represented one of twentieth-century Southeast Asia's most visually striking expressions of religious syncretism and radical pacifism. Nam preached that the Vietnam War was a spiritual catastrophe born of theological fragmentation and human egoism, which could only be healed by fusing the "Three Teachings" of East Asia (Buddhism, Confucianism, Daoism) with Christianity.

The architecture of Phoenix Island reflected this eclectic synthesis: its main open-air sanctuary featured nine dragon columns representing the Mekong Delta branches, flanked by large statues of Gautama Buddha and Jesus Christ standing side by side beneath an immense metal peace tower. Devotees wore blue robes, kept long hair, and engaged in regular prayer sessions blending Buddhist mantras, Catholic hymns, and Daoist chants. The tradition is classified historically as an extinct syncretic new religious movement, though Phoenix Island survives today as a popular cultural and tourist destination in Bến Tre.
