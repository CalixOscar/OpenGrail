# Bửu Sơn Kỳ Hương

## Historical context

Bửu Sơn Kỳ Hương ("Strange Fragrance from the Precious Mountain") was founded in 1849 during a devastating cholera epidemic in the western Mekong Delta by Đoàn Minh Huyên (1807–1856), revered by followers as *Phật Thầy Tây An* (Buddha Master of the Western Peace). Establishing himself at the Tây An Pagoda at the foot of Mount Sam in An Giang Province, Huyên distributed herbal remedies, paper amulets, and moral counsel to thousands of afflicted peasants, refusing all fees and donations.

As his following expanded, Huyên dispatched disciples into the wild, malaria-ridden wetlands of the Seven Mountains (*Thất Sơn*) and the Plain of Reeds to establish agricultural pioneer settlements (*trại ruộng*). These monastic-agrarian communes combined collective farming with self-defense and Buddhist meditation. Following the French conquest of Cochinchina in the 1860s, Bửu Sơn Kỳ Hương communities became vital centers of anticolonial resistance, inspiring subsequent southern Vietnamese religious movements, notably Tứ Ân Hiếu Nghĩa and Hòa Hảo Buddhism.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Bửu Sơn Kỳ Hương introduced a major Reformation-like shift within Vietnamese Buddhism, replacing elaborate monastic rituals, costly temple donations, and deity statues with accessible domestic piety. Central to its theology is the doctrine of *Tu Nhân Học Phật* (cultivating one's humanity while studying the Buddha), asserting that lay moral fulfillment in daily life is the primary vehicle for spiritual liberation.

The movement's liturgical hallmark is the *trần điều*—a plain rectangular piece of red or brown cloth hung above the central home altar in place of statues or painted images of the Buddha. The cloth symbolizes the formless, universal essence of Buddha-nature (*Phật tính*) and the undivided blood unity of humanity. Practice centers on fulfilling the *Tứ Ân* (Four Great Debts of Gratitude): gratitude to parents and ancestors, gratitude to the nation and its defenders, gratitude to the Three Jewels of Buddhism, and gratitude to humanity and fellow beings.
