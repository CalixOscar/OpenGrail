# Kurozumikyō

## Historical context

Kurozumikyō was founded on the winter solstice of 1814 by Kurozumi Munetada (1780–1850), a Shinto priest at the Imamura Shrine in Okayama. After falling critically ill with tuberculosis following the bereavement of both parents, Munetada experienced a profound mystical awakening during a dawn prayer to the rising sun, in which he felt filled with divine vitality (*yōki*) and experienced spontaneous healing. He began ministering to local samurai and peasants, transmitting healing through prayer, vital breath dispensation, and ethical instruction.

During the late Edo and early Meiji periods, the movement expanded rapidly across western Japan. Following the Meiji Restoration and the government's centralization of state shrine administration, Kurozumikyō secured official recognition in 1876 as an independent Shinto sect (*Kyōha Shintō*), establishing its central sanctuary on Mount Shintō in Okayama, where it continues active worship and institutional life today.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Kurozumikyō interprets the central Shinto solar deity, Amaterasu Ōmikami, not merely as the mythic ancestress of the imperial line, but as the supreme, all-encompassing parent spirit of the cosmos (*Oyagami*). The tradition teaches that human beings are direct offshoots (*bunrei*) of this cosmic solar life. Human illness and social distress are understood to arise from gloomy, closed, or self-pitying mindsets (*inki*), which sever the flow of divine life energy.

The hallmark ritual practice is *nippai*, the dawn salutation performed facing the rising sun, during which devotees inhale the morning air and sunlight into the abdomen to revitalize mind and body. Unlike traditional shrine Shinto, which historically maintained institutional boundaries between priesthood and lay worshippers, Kurozumikyō developed a participatory congregational structure emphasizing lay healing, personal moral transformation (*makoto*), and mutual pastoral care.
