# Sūkyō Mahikari

## Historical context

The origins of Sūkyō Mahikari trace to Okada Yoshikazu (1901–1974, known by the religious title Okada Kōtama), a former military officer and disciple of Sekai Kyūsei-kyō (Church of World Messianity). On February 27, 1959, Okada experienced a revelatory vision in which the Supreme Creator God commanded him to proclaim the advent of the baptism by fire and transmit divine True Light (*Mahikari*) to the world, establishing the initial organization Sekai Mahikari Bunmei Kyōdan.

Following Okada Kōtama's death in 1974, a leadership dispute led to the establishment of two separate organizations: Sekai Mahikari Bunmei Kyōdan and Sūkyō Mahikari. The latter was registered in 1978 under the leadership of his adopted daughter, Okada Keishu (1929–2002), who served as the second spiritual leader (*Oshienushisama*). In 1984, Sūkyō Mahikari completed the grand Suza World Shrine (*Sekai Sō Honzan*) in Takayama, Gifu Prefecture. Leadership subsequently passed to Okada Kōō in 2002, overseeing a worldwide network of centers across Asia, Europe, Africa, Oceania, and the Americas.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Sūkyō Mahikari teaches that all human beings, regardless of nationality or religious background, are children of the single Supreme Creator God (*Su-God*). The movement explains disease, natural disasters, and social discord as the manifestation of spiritual, mental, and physical toxins, often aggravated by earthbound ancestral spirits (*hontai-shō*) in need of spiritual light.

The core practice is *Mahikari-no-Waza* (the Art of True Light), performed by practitioners wearing a sacred pendant (*Omitama*) that connects them to the divine source. A practitioner raises their hand and radiates light toward specific spiritual energy points on the recipient's body, especially the forehead (primary point) and head, to dissolve spiritual cloudiness and physical impurities. Alongside light transmission, the tradition emphasizes *Yōkō* natural agriculture (farming without chemical fertilizers or pesticides), filial piety, and gratitude toward ancestors through dedicated home ancestral altars.
