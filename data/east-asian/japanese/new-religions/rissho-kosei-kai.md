# Risshō Kōsei-kai

## Historical context

Risshō Kōsei-kai was established on March 5, 1938, by Niwano Nikkyō (1906–1999) and Naganuma Myōkō (1889–1957). Both had been active branch leaders in Reiyūkai, but they became convinced that Reiyūkai’s exclusive focus on ancestral memorialization was insufficient without a deeper, systematic study of core Buddhist doctrine and the full philosophy of the *Lotus Sutra*. Starting with a modest group of thirty members in Tokyo, the movement grew rapidly in the post-World War II period to become one of the largest and most influential lay Buddhist organizations in Japan, counting millions of members.

Under Niwano's visionary leadership, Risshō Kōsei-kai established the Great Sacred Hall (*Dai-Seidō*) in Suginami, Tokyo, and gained international prominence for its commitment to interreligious cooperation. Niwano attended the Second Vatican Council as an invited guest of Pope Paul VI in 1965, co-founded the World Conference of Religions for Peace (WCRP / Religions for Peace) in 1970, and was awarded the Templeton Prize for Progress in Religion in 1979. Leadership transitioned to his son, Niwano Nichikō, continuing the organization's humanitarian and peace-building mission.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Risshō Kōsei-kai anchors its theology in the *Threefold Lotus Sutra*—comprising the *Sutra of Innumerable Meanings*, the *Lotus Sutra* (*Myōhō Renge Kyō*), and the *Sutra of Meditation on the Bodhisattva Universal Virtue*. Unlike some Nichiren movements that adopt an exclusive or polemical stance toward other traditions (*shakubuku*), Risshō Kōsei-kai practices *shōju* (gentle embrace and accommodation), actively pursuing dialogue and collaborative social action with Christians, Muslims, Hindus, and other Buddhist lineages worldwide.

At the grassroots community level, religious life centers upon *hōza* (Dharma circles), where groups of ten to fifteen members sit in a circle on tatami mats to share everyday trials—marital difficulties, health concerns, parenting issues, and financial anxieties. Guided by experienced leaders, participants analyze their situations through basic Buddhist frameworks like the Twelve Links of Dependent Origination and the Eightfold Path, cultivating self-awareness, forgiveness, and personal responsibility. Members also perform daily home morning and evening sutra recitations and maintain ancestral veneration altars.
