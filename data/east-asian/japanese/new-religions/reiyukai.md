# Reiyūkai

## Historical context

Reiyūkai ("Spiritual Friendship Association") was established in Tokyo in 1925 by Kubo Kakutarō (1892–1944), a technician in the Imperial Household Agency, and his sister-in-law Kotani Kimi (1901–1971), who became the movement’s dynamic spiritual president. Kubo had studied Nichiren Buddhism and concluded that the social crises of modernizing Japan stemmed from the decay of traditional family filial piety and the neglect of ancestor memorialization caused by an over-reliance on professional temple priesthoods.

Formally inaugurated in 1930, Reiyūkai expanded swiftly among urban working-class and merchant families across Tokyo and surrounding prefectures. By the mid-twentieth century, it had become one of the most influential parent organizations among Japanese new religions, spawning numerous independent lay Buddhist offshoots, including Risshō Kōsei-kai, Bussho Gonenkai, and Myōchikai. Reiyūkai established its grand headquarters and the Mirokusan sanctuary on the Izu Peninsula and continues an international presence.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Reiyūkai represents a radical democratization of Japanese Buddhist funerary and memorial rites. In traditional Japanese Buddhism, memorial services (*kuyō*) were the exclusive domain of ordained Buddhist monks who possessed exclusive authority to bestow posthumous Buddhist names (*kaimyō*). Reiyūkai empowered lay householders—particularly women—to transcribe their own ancestral registers (*kakochō*), encompassing not only the paternal lineage but also the maternal lineage and extended relations, and to transfer spiritual merit directly through home recitation.

The central ritual practice is the daily chanting of the *Blue Sutra*, a compilation edited by Kubo Kakutarō containing excerpts from the *Threefold Lotus Sutra* and the *Mirokukyō* (Maitreya Sutra). Reiyūkai pioneered the institution of *hōza* ("Dharma circle"), in which small groups of believers gather informally in members' homes to discuss daily life difficulties, marital friction, illness, and character flaws. Within *hōza*, participants interpret their personal troubles as karmic reflections of unresolved ancestral distress, working through mutual confession, ethical guidance, and spiritual support to restore domestic harmony and social well-being.
