# Sarnaism

## Historical context

Sarnaism (known natively as *Sarna Dharma*, *Sari Dharam*, or *Kherwal Dharam*) is the indigenous religious tradition of millions of Adivasi (indigenous tribal) peoples inhabiting the Chota Nagpur Plateau and surrounding forested regions of eastern and central India—predominantly the Munda, Oraon (Kurukh), Ho, Santal, Kharia, and Bhumij communities across Jharkhand, Odisha, West Bengal, Bihar, and Chhattisgarh.

Historically rooted in ancient Austroasiatic and Dravidian forest-dwelling cultures, Sarna communities preserved their customary laws, village governance systems (*Parha* / *Pancha*), and sacred rituals through centuries of contact with regional kingdoms and British colonial rule. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, amidst missionary expansion and demographic encroachment, Adivasi leaders such as Birsa Munda (1875–1900) mobilized powerful socio-religious movements defending tribal autonomy and sacred land rights. In contemporary India, Sarna adherents maintain an active nationwide movement demanding the formal recognition of a separate "Sarna Code" in the national census, rejecting administrative categorization as Hindu and asserting constitutional recognition for their distinct indigenous religion.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Sarna theology is grounded in an ecological animism that honors the sacredness of the natural world without idols, temples, or a Brahminical caste hierarchy. The spiritual architecture of Sarnaism is defined by several core elements:
- *The Sacred Grove (Sarna / Jaherthan)*: Every traditional village maintains an uncultivated patch of ancestral primary forest, predominantly composed of *sal* trees (*Shorea robusta*), where village guardian spirits dwell. Cutting trees or desecrating the Sarna is strictly taboo.
- *Singbonga and the Bongas*: Veneration is directed toward *Singbonga* (the supreme creator God, personified in the life-giving light of the Sun), *Marang Buru* (the Great Mountain deity), *Jaher Era* (the Lady of the Sacred Grove), and lineage ancestral spirits (*Orabonga* / *Hapramko*).
- *The Pahan (Priest)*: Village rituals are officiated by the *Pahan* (or *Naike*), a hereditary village priest chosen through divination, assisted by the *Pujar*, while social governance is directed by the secular headman (*Munda* / *Manjhi*).

Major liturgical observances follow the agrarian and forest calendar:
- *Sarhul* (or *Baha Parab*): The most sacred spring festival, celebrating the blooming of the sal tree and the mystical marriage of the Earth (*Dharti*) with the Sun (*Singbonga*). No new agricultural activity or forest gathering may occur before Sarhul blessings are completed.
- *Karma*: An autumn festival dedicated to youth, fertility, and labor, centered on the ritual planting of a sacred branch of the *karam* tree (*Nauclea parvifolia*) in the village dancing ground (*Akhra*).

Sarnaism explicitly distinguishes itself from Hinduism. It possesses no concept of caste pollution, rejects the authority of the Vedas and Hindu pantheon, does not employ image worship or temple liturgies, and maintains a direct, egalitarian relationship with ancestral spirits and the living environment.
