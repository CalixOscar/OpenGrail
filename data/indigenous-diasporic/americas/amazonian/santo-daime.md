# Santo Daime

## Historical context

Santo Daime emerged in the 1930s in the western Brazilian Amazonian state of Acre, founded by Raimundo Irineu Serra (known as Mestre Irineu, 1892–1971), an Afro-Brazilian rubber tapper from Maranhão. While working in the borderlands between Brazil, Peru, and Bolivia, Irineu was introduced by indigenous and mestizo healers (vegetalistas / ayahuasqueros) to the sacred Amazonian beverage prepared from the *Banisteriopsis caapi* vine and *Psychotria viridis* leaves. During visionary experiences in the forest, Irineu received revelations from a divine female entity identified as the Queen of the Forest (Rainha da Floresta), associated syncretically with the Virgin Mary (Nossa Senhora da Conceição).

Under her direction, Irineu established a formal religious doctrine centered on the sacrament, which he named *Daime* (derived from the Portuguese phrase *Dai-me amor, Dai-me luz*, "Give me love, Give me light"). The original center, Alto Santo, was founded in Rio Branco. Following Mestre Irineu's death in 1971, the movement developed several branches, notably the lineage established by Sebastião Mota de Melo (Padrinho Sebastião), which incorporated mediumistic healing sessions (incorporation works inspired by Umbanda and Kardecism) and founded the ecovillage Céu do Mapiá in the Amazonas rainforest. From the 1980s onward, Santo Daime spread internationally across Europe, North America, and Japan.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Santo Daime is characterized by its musical, communal liturgy. Rather than relying on prose sermons, doctrinal teachings are transmitted through *hinários* (hymnals)—poetic verses received by church leaders in visionary states set to recognizable folk rhythms (waltz, march, and mazurka). Ritual gatherings are categorized into distinct "works" (*trabalhos*): *Bailados* (festive all-night dances where congregants wear formal white uniforms and maracas called *maracás* to maintain a collective rhythm), *Concentrações* (silent meditation sessions for introspection), *Missas* (masses for the deceased), and *Curas* (healing sessions).

The tradition represents a complex synthesis of multiple spiritual streams. From Amazonian shamanism it inherits the master plant sacrament and deep ecological cosmology; from folk Catholicism it draws its calendar of feast days (Saint John, Christmas, Holy Week), prayers, and iconography; from Kardecist Spiritism it borrows the concepts of spiritual evolution through successive incarnations, charity, and mediumistic alignment; and from Afro-Brazilian religiosity it integrates trance healing and rhythmic movement.

In Brazil, the religious use of Daime was formally legitimized following rigorous multidisciplinary investigations by the Federal Council of Narcotics (CONFEN) in the 1980s and confirmed by the National Council on Drug Policies (CONAD) in 2004, recognizing the sacrament as an integral expression of constitutionally protected religious freedom. International branches have similarly engaged with legal and human rights frameworks to secure statutory exemptions for sacramental practice.
