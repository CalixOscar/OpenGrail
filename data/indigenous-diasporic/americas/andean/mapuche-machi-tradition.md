# Mapuche Cosmovision & Machi Traditions

## Historical context

The Mapuche people of Wallmapu (encompassing ancestral territories across southern Chile and southwestern Argentina) developed a resilient and sophisticated spiritual system that maintained its autonomy through centuries of resistance against both the Inca Empire and the Spanish Empire. Even following the late nineteenth-century military campaigns (the Chilean "Pacification of Araucanía" and the Argentine "Conquest of the Desert"), Mapuche spiritual structures, language (Mapudungun), and healing institutions persisted with strong vitality.

Central to Mapuche religious life is the *machi*—a spiritual leader, herbalist, diviner, and cosmic mediator (most frequently a woman or a person embodying complementary gender dualities). Machis are called to their vocation through serious illnesses, prophetic dreams (*pewma*), or ancestral visions (*perimontun*). Today, machis continue to serve as essential medical, judicial, and spiritual leaders within rural communities (*lof*) and expanding urban Mapuche populations.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Mapuche cosmology envisions a vertical, multi-layered cosmos consisting of three primary realms:
- *Wenu Mapu* (the upper celestial realm of good spirits, creator deities, and ancestral light)
- *Nag Mapu* (the earthly visible realm where humans, plants, animals, and nature spirits co-exist)
- *Minche Mapu* (the lower underworld realm associated with chaotic, dark, or destructive forces)

At the pinnacle of the pantheon stands *Ngünechen* ("Ruler of the People"), conceived in quadripartite complementarity as simultaneously Old Man, Old Woman, Young Man, and Young Woman. Spiritual life is governed by *Admapu*—the ancestral code of ethics, customary law, and ecological balance. Every feature of the landscape—mountains, springs, forests, and volcanoes—is animated by a resident spirit (*ngen*), requiring ritual permission and offerings of tobacco, sacred muday (fermented grain beverage), and prayers before gathering plants or altering the land.

The central ritual apparatus of the machi is the *rewe*—a stepped sacred wooden pole carved from the canelo tree (*foye*) representing the axis mundi—and the *kultrún*, a sacred hemispherical drum painted with a cosmological quadrant design representing the four cardinal directions and cosmic seasons. During the *machitun* (healing ritual), the machi ascends the *rewe* in an ecstatic trance (*küymin*) induced by drumming to dialogue with ancestral spirits (*pullü*) and extract illness-causing spiritual blockages (*wekufü*). At the communal level, the *Ngillatun* is an elaborate multi-day agrarian ceremony gathering entire lineages to give thanks, ensure fertility, and maintain equilibrium between the community and cosmic powers.
