# Indian Shaker Church

## Historical context

The Indian Shaker Church originated in late 1882 at Mud Bay, on the southern shores of Puget Sound near Olympia, Washington, through the religious experiences of John Slocum (Squaxin Island / Coast Salish) and his wife Mary Slocum. According to tradition, John Slocum fell gravely ill and was believed to have died; during his death trance, he experienced a vision in which he was met by an angel and shown heaven, but was turned back because of his unbaptized condition and moral shortcomings, receiving a mandate to return and preach repentance, sobriety, and clean living to Native people. Slocum began holding services in a small church built at Mud Bay, drawing followers from diverse Coast Salish communities.

When John fell severely ill again around a year later, Mary Slocum experienced an overwhelming spiritual seizure while praying over his body. She began to weep, tremble violently, and lay hands upon him; John subsequently recovered, and this trembling (*the shake*) was recognized by the community as a powerful gift from the Holy Spirit (*Kwathat*) for healing the sick and purifying the spirit.

From Mud Bay, the movement spread rapidly along intertribal trade, fishing, and kinship networks throughout western Washington, Oregon (including Siletz and Warm Springs), northwestern California (Hupa, Yurok, Tolowa, and Karuk communities), and northward into British Columbia and Southeast Alaska among Tlingit and Haida peoples. Federal Indian agents and white missionary organizations viewed the church with severe hostility, branding the shaking as "heathenish frenzy." Bureau of Indian Affairs officials outlawed Shaker services in the 1890s and repeatedly arrested and jailed John Slocum, Mud Bay Louis, and other Shaker ministers. Despite this official suppression, the church persisted clandestinely and solidified its legal protection by formally incorporating under Washington State law in 1910 as the Indian Shaker Church of Washington (later the Indian Shaker Church of North America). The church remains an active, sovereign Indigenous Christian denomination today.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Despite its name, the Indian Shaker Church is entirely independent of, and historically unrelated to, the United Society of Believers in Christ's Second Appearing (the Shakers of Ann Lee, founded in eighteenth-century England and America). The term "Shaker" was applied externally by observers and later adopted officially by the church due to the central phenomenon of bodily trembling during worship.

The church represents a distinctive and resilient syncretism of Indigenous Northwest Coast cosmology and Christian forms. Worship is conducted in modest white wooden chapels dominated by an altar bearing three burning candles (symbolizing the Trinity) and a wooden cross. Worshipers, dressed in white robes or shirts, sing repetitive, hypnotic chants in Indigenous languages and English without instrumental accompaniment, accompanied only by the sharp, rhythmic ringing of heavy brass handbells held in each hand and the synchronized stamping of feet.

During healing ceremonies, ministers and congregants surround the sick person, singing and ringing bells until the *shake* seizes them. Practitioners use their trembling hands and white ribbons to brush away sickness, spiritual pollution, and sorrow, capturing negative energies in their hands and releasing them upward toward heaven or casting them out. The church enforces strict codes of personal morality, demanding complete abstinence from alcohol, tobacco, gambling, and traditional shamanic sorcery (*Indian doctoring*), providing a supportive, community-based alternative to institutionalized colonial churches and governmental temperance campaigns. While open to respectful visitors, the church maintains sacred protocols concerning the privacy of its healing testimonies and inner spiritual operations.
