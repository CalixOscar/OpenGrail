# Dream Dance (Drum Dance)

## Historical context

The Dream Dance (also known as the Drum Dance, Big Drum Religion, or *Bwaanidewe'igan*) emerged during the mid-1870s (c. 1876–1878) in the Upper Midwest borderlands of Minnesota, Wisconsin, and the Dakotas. The movement originated from the visionary experience of a Dakota woman, commonly remembered as Tailfeather Woman (Wiyaka Waste Win). According to oral accounts, she miraculously escaped a massacre of her camp by United States cavalry forces by hiding for several days submerged in a marsh beneath water lily pads. During her concealment, she was visited by the Great Spirit (*Wakan Tanka* / *Gichi-Manidoo*), who showed her a large ceremonial drum and gave her sacred songs, dance protocols, and moral teachings.

Tailfeather Woman was instructed to build a large drum and take it to the Anishinaabe (Ojibwe/Chippewa)—historical adversaries of the Dakota—as a gift of peace, brotherhood, and spiritual renewal. In 1877–1878, Dakota delegates presented the first drum to Anishinaabe bands in central Minnesota, who embraced the movement and passed it rapidly to the Menominee, Potawatomi, Ho-Chunk (Winnebago), and neighboring Great Lakes nations. The ceremonial presentation of drums effectively transformed centuries of regional warfare into enduring intertribal diplomatic and kinship alliances.

Despite surveillance and attempts at suppression by Bureau of Indian Affairs agents who sought to ban Indian dancing in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the Big Drum religion survived. It continues as a living, highly revered ceremonial tradition maintained by dedicated Drum societies across Anishinaabe and Menominee communities in Minnesota, Wisconsin, and Ontario.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

At the center of the tradition is the Big Drum itself, regarded not merely as a musical instrument, but as a living sacred person, an altar, and a direct conduit to the spiritual realm. Drums are constructed according to meticulous ceremonial guidelines, traditionally decorated with sacred beadwork, ribbons, fur, and four painted quadrants representing cosmological directions and spiritual powers. Each drum is stewarded by a formalized society of officers, typically including a Drum Owner/Keeper, singers, drum heaters, pipe lighters, and speakers who hold designated responsibilities.

Ceremonies involve the recitation of the Tailfeather Woman narrative, tobacco offerings, communal feast offerings to the spirits and ancestors, and the performance of specific song cycles accompanied by disciplined ring dances. The tradition mandates strict moral codes, demanding peaceful behavior, mutual respect, family responsibility, and complete sobriety—the drum forbids alcohol and anger in its presence.

A defining feature of the Dream Dance is the formal ritual transfer of the Drum. A host community constructs a new drum, trains in its songs, and then travels to another nation to ceremonially gift the drum along with horses, blankets, beadwork, and sacred gifts, creating lasting bonds of reciprocal obligation and peace.

Early anthropological accounts from the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries (such as those by Samuel Barrett and Walter Hoffman) frequently recorded secret songs and ritual procedures without community consent. Contemporary scholars and Indigenous practitioners emphasize that specific ceremonial protocols, medicine songs, and drum teachings belong to initiated societies and are not intended for public external reproduction. The Dream Dance remains a profound testament to sovereign Indigenous diplomacy, healing historical intertribal wounds through sacred art, song, and spiritual discipline.
