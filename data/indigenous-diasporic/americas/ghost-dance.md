# Ghost Dance Movements

## Historical context

Two related prophetic dance movements spread through western North American networks, first around the Paiute prophet Wodziwob in 1870 and later around the Northern Paiute prophet Wovoka after 1889. Communities adapted teachings to their own losses, hopes, and ceremonies. The Lakota form became linked in public memory to the 1890 Wounded Knee massacre, where U.S. troops killed hundreds, but the movement was never exclusively Lakota.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Dancing, songs, moral renewal, reunion with the dead, restored land, and the ending of colonial suffering received locally different interpretations. Wovoka's language drew partly on Christian encounter while remaining an Indigenous prophetic response. Calling the movement irrational “millenarian frenzy” repeats colonial framing. Its history foregrounds communication among sovereign peoples and the violent conditions under which religious hope was criminalized.
