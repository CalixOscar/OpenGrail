# Santa Muerte

## Historical context

Veneration of skeletal personifications of death in Mexico has deep syncretic roots connecting pre-Columbian Mesoamerican mortuary deities—notably the Aztec underworld rulers Mictlantecuhtli and Mictecacihuatl—with European Catholic devotions such as the *Danse Macabre* and the veneration of Spanish colonial skeletal figures (such as San Pascualito in Chiapas). The earliest documentary references to clandestine Santa Muerte devotion appear in late eighteenth-century inquisitorial records in central Mexico (c. 1795), where ecclesiastical authorities suppressed rural shrines where indigenous communities prayed to skeletal effigies for love and protection.

For most of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, the devotion survived as a private, domestic, and largely underground folk tradition passed down through families, particularly in Veracruz, Hidalgo, and Mexico City. The tradition shifted into rapid public prominence in 2001 when Enriqueta Romero established the first public street altar in the Tepito neighborhood of Mexico City. Over the subsequent two decades, Santa Muerte developed into one of the fastest-growing religious movements in the Americas, spreading across Mexico and extensive diaspora communities in the United States and Central America, with millions of devotees across diverse socioeconomic strata.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Santa Muerte is envisioned as a female skeletal figure robed in flowing garments, carrying an iconic scythe (symbolizing the severance of negative energies and the inevitability of death), a globe (representing dominion over all earthly existence), scales of justice, and sometimes an owl denoting nocturnal wisdom. Statues are color-coded according to the petition: white for purification and peace, red for love and passion, gold for financial prosperity, green for legal justice, and black for total protection against physical harm and supernatural attack.

Devotional practice relies heavily on Catholic ritual structures, including the recitation of rosaries, novenas, and lighting of votive candles, complemented by offerings of tobacco smoke, tequila, perfume, sweets, and fresh flowers. Devotees often maintain a simultaneous Catholic identity, frequently praying first to God or Jesus Christ for permission before petitioning the saint of death.

Sensationalist media accounts have frequently reduced Santa Muerte to a "narco-saint" associated narrowly with organized crime and cartel violence. Scholarly ethnographies emphasize that while the saint's non-judgmental acceptance does make her popular among those working in dangerous, illicit, or heavily stigmatized environments (including prisoners, police officers, and sex workers), the overwhelming majority of her millions of adherents are ordinary working-class families, market vendors, single mothers, LGBTQ+ individuals, and youth seeking health, employment, family harmony, and protection against state failure and economic precarity. The Roman Catholic Church explicitly condemns the devotion as blasphemous and spiritually dangerous, denying Santa Muerte saintly status and maintaining a strict theological boundary against personified death worship.
