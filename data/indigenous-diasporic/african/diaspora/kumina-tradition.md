# Kumina

## Historical context

Kumina is an Afro-Jamaican religious tradition centered in the eastern parishes of Jamaica, especially Saint Thomas, Portland, and Saint Mary. Unlike older Caribbean traditions that evolved during early transatlantic plantation slavery (such as Jamaican Myal or Haitian Vodou), Kumina was introduced comparatively late: between the 1840s and 1860s, following the abolition of slavery in the British Empire, several thousand indentured African contract laborers from the Congo Basin (principally Kikongo and Kimbundu speakers) arrived in Jamaica.

These Congolese immigrants settled in close-knit communities in Saint Thomas, where they preserved their ancestral language, musical repertoires, and cosmological structures with remarkable fidelity. Led by revered community elders known as Kumina "Kings" and "Queens" (such as the famed twentieth-century ritual leader Queen Imogene "Man Dag" Kennedy), Kumina maintained an autonomous ritual space that heavily influenced broader Jamaican cultural forms, including Revivalism and early Rastafari drumming.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

The theological framework of Kumina recognizes *King Zombi* (derived from the Kikongo *Nzambi a Mpungu*, the Supreme Sky God) as the supreme creator, who is surrounded by a hierarchy of spirits categorized into sky gods, earthbound spirits, and ancestral spirits (*Nkuyu* or *Zombis*). Kumina practitioners distinguish between two classes of ancestors:
- Ancestors of African birth (*African Zombie* / *Old African Spirit*), who possess formidable ritual and magical power and speak archaic Kikongo.
- Creole ancestors (*Creole Zombie*), recently deceased family members who communicate in Jamaican Patois to provide direct family guidance.

Ritual sessions take place during critical life transitions: funerals and memorial ceremonies (specifically the *Nine-Night* and the *Tombing* ceremony held a year after death to send the spirit peacefully to the ancestral realm), healing rites, and festive thanksgiving events. Ceremonies are powered by a sacred drum battery consisting of:
- The *Kbandu*: A large, deep-toned base drum played while straddled by the drummer, maintaining the foundational ostinato rhythm.
- The *Playing Cast*: A smaller, higher-pitched lead drum played with intricate polyrhythmic improvisations to call down the spirits.

During a Kumina dance, the spirit mounts the dancer in an altered state of consciousness called *Myal* (the possession state), causing the dancer to perform rapid, flat-footed turns and leaps while channeling prophecies, cleansing physical ailments, and dispensing ancestral wisdom.
