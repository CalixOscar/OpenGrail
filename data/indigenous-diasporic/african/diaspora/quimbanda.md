# Quimbanda

## Historical context

Quimbanda developed in urban Brazil (principally in Rio de Janeiro, São Paulo, and Porto Alegre) during the early decades of the twentieth century. It coalesced alongside Umbanda as part of the broader flourishing of Afro-Brazilian spiritual expressions following the abolition of slavery in 1888. The name derives from the Kimbundu word *kimbanda*, designating a traditional Central African (Kongo-Angola) healer, diviner, and spiritual mediator.

While mainstream Umbanda sought social legitimacy among the Brazilian middle class by emphasizing Kardecist Spiritist morality, Catholic saint syncretism, and "white table" mediumship (the Right-Hand Line or *Linha da Direita*, featuring *caboclos* and *pretos velhos*), Quimbanda developed as an autonomous ritual tradition focused explicitly on the Left-Hand Line (*Linha da Esquerda*). In recent decades, many traditional houses (*terreiros*) have re-asserted Quimbanda's complete organizational and philosophical independence from Umbanda, embracing its distinct Bantu roots and esoteric foundations.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Colonial, missionary, and sensationalist observers frequently mischaracterized Quimbanda as "black magic" or "devil worship," imposing a Christian-derived moral dualism onto an African-derived cosmology that does not operate on concepts of absolute evil. In Quimbanda's own theological framework, the universe is governed by dynamic balance, where constructive and destructive forces are complementary aspects of cosmic order.

The central entities venerated in Quimbanda are:
- *Exus*: Male spirits who lived intensely on earth—often as warriors, wanderers, rogues, tricksters, and philosophers—who have attained spiritual wisdom and understand the full spectrum of human desires, challenges, and suffering. They preside over crossroads (*encruzilhadas*), forests, and thresholds, serving as tireless protectors and enforcers of spiritual justice.
- *Pombagiras*: Female counterparts of Exus representing feminine empowerment, sensual autonomy, emotional insight, and magical authority. They work through specific domains such as cemeteries, crossroads, and rivers.

Rituals in a Quimbanda *terreiro* take place during night sessions (*giras*). Mediums enter altered states of consciousness to incorporate these spirits, who offer pragmatic counsel, perform energetic cleansings (*descarregos*), and resolve complex life problems. Rites employ *pontos riscados* (complex chalk sigils incorporating tridents, stars, and arrows that anchor spiritual force fields) and *pontos cantados* (percussive chants). Offerings (*ebós* and *oferendas*) typically consist of roasted meats, peppers, farofa, cigars, cachaça (for Exus), and champagne or roses (for Pombagiras), designed to feed and align the spirit's vital force (*axé*).
