# Cult of Despoina

## Historical context

The sanctuary of Despoina was located at Lykosoura on the slopes of Mount Lykaion in southwestern Arcadia, a site celebrated by the travel-writer Pausanias as the "first and oldest city founded upon the earth." Cult activity at the sanctuary dates archaeologically to at least the sixth century BCE and continued uninterrupted through the Roman imperial era until the late fourth century CE.

In the Hellenistic period (c. 2nd century BCE), the sanctuary was monumentalized with a great Doric temple and an elaborate *megaron* altar hall housing a colossal marble sculptural group carved by Damophon of Messene. The group depicted enthroned colossal statues of Demeter and Despoina holding scepters, flanked by standing figures of Artemis and the Titan Anytos, who was said to have raised Despoina.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

The Arcadian myth of Despoina's birth preserves an archaic, equine theological stratum quite distinct from the canonical Panhellenic myth of Persephone (Kore) and Zeus. According to local myth, while Demeter was wandering in grief searching for her daughter, Poseidon pursued her; Demeter transformed herself into a mare to escape, but Poseidon transformed into a stallion and mated with her. From this union were born the wondrous talking stallion Arion and a daughter whose personal name was forbidden to be spoken to the uninitiated, known publicly only by the reverent cult title *Despoina* ("The Mistress" or "The Lady").

The sacred laws (*lex sacra*) of Lykosoura inscribed on stone tablets set strict ritual requirements for visitors: prohibiting the wearing of purple robes, gold jewelry, embroidered clothing, or hair bound in plaits, and requiring worshippers to enter barefoot. Terracotta figurines found at the site depict human celebrants wearing animal masks (such as horses, rams, and pigs) engaged in ritual dance. Unlike the pan-Mediterranean prominence of Eleusis, the cult of Despoina remained a deeply localized, secretive mystery sanctuary that protected the archaic identity of Arcadian religion against panhellenic homogenization.
