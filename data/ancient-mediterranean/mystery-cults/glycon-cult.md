# Cult of Glycon

## Historical context

The cult of Glycon was established around 150 CE in the Black Sea port city of Abonoteichos in Paphlagonia (modern İnebolu, Turkey) by the charismatic mystic and prophet Alexander. Proclaiming that the healing god Asclepius had reincarnated in serpentine form, Alexander staged the dramatic birth of a newborn snake from a buried goose egg in the foundations of a new temple, followed by the revelation of the full-grown serpent Glycon, crowned with blonde human-like hair.

The shrine rapidly became one of the most prominent oracle centers in the Roman East, attracting thousands of pilgrims from across Asia Minor, Thrace, and Rome itself, including high-ranking Roman senators such as the consul Publius Mummius Sisenna Rutilianus (who married Alexander's daughter). Emperor Antoninus Pius authorized the city to rename itself Ionopolis in honor of the cult and issue official municipal coinage bearing the image of Glycon. The cult maintained widespread popularity across the Black Sea and Moesian frontier until the mid-third century CE.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

The primary literary account of the cult comes from Lucian of Samosata's scathing satirical treatise *Alexander the False Prophet* (*Alexander seu Pseudomantis*), written shortly after Alexander's death (c. 170 CE). Lucian depicts Alexander as an unprincipled charlatan who manufactured a glove puppet serpent head made of linen and horsehair, used heated needles to unseal query scrolls, and hired confederates to stage fraudulent miracles.

However, modern historiography and archaeology provide a critical counterweight to Lucian's polemical hostility. Inscriptions discovered throughout the Danubian provinces, Moesia, and Rome (including dedications by Roman legionary commanders and civil magistrates) alongside a famous second-century marble cult statue discovered at Tomis (Constanța, Romania) demonstrate that Glycon was worshipped sincerely across multiple social strata as a protective, plague-averting savior god. Glycon's cult exemplifies how classical Greek healing traditions and snake symbology could be reinvented to meet the spiritual anxieties and epidemic crises of the Antonine Roman Empire.
