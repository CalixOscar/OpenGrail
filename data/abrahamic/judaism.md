# Judaism

## Historical genesis

Judaism developed from the religions of ancient Israel and Judah rather than appearing at a single founding moment. The Hebrew Bible preserves memories of mobile pastoral communities, monarchy, temple sacrifice, prophetic criticism, conquest, exile, and restoration. Archaeology and textual study indicate a long movement from a West Semitic religious environment toward the exclusive worship of YHWH. The Babylonian exile in the sixth century BCE and the Persian-period restoration were especially important for the formation, collection, and interpretation of authoritative texts.

The destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE did not simply end one religion and begin another. Diverse Jewish groups had already organized life through scripture, prayer, legal interpretation, pilgrimage, sacrifice, and local assemblies in different proportions. Rabbinic Judaism gradually became a particularly influential framework through the Mishnah, the Palestinian and Babylonian Talmuds, and later legal and philosophical traditions. Karaite, Samaritan, mystical, and other Jewish communities complicate any single linear genealogy.

## Ideas and practice

The covenant between God and Israel, Torah, ethical obligation, sacred time, collective memory, and argument over interpretation are central themes. Jewish thought ranges from apophatic philosophy to Kabbalistic accounts of divine emanation, and from strict naturalism to robust messianic expectation. Practice is equally important: Sabbath, festivals, dietary rules, prayer, study, charity, and life-cycle observances make tradition tangible in community.

## Comparative notes

Some scholars argue that Persian-period contact with Zoroastrian traditions sharpened Jewish ideas about angels, resurrection, final judgment, and cosmic opposition. Contact is historically plausible, but the direction and scale of transmission remain debated; related ideas may also reflect internal development or a wider West Asian environment.
