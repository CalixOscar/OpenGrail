# Black Hebrew Israelites

## Historical context

The Black Hebrew Israelite movement originated in the late nineteenth and early
twentieth centuries in the United States during the era of Jim Crow segregation and the
Great Migration. The movement's earliest documented pioneers were William Saunders
Crowdy (1847–1908), a formerly enslaved runaway who founded the Church of God and
Saints of Christ in Lawrence, Kansas, in 1896 following visionary revelations, and
Frank S. Cherry, who established the Church of the Living God in Chattanooga,
Tennessee, around 1886.

During the Harlem Renaissance of the 1920s and 1930s, the movement diversified
extensively in northern urban centers. Rabbi Wentworth Arthur Matthew (1892–1973),
influenced by Marcus Garvey's Universal Negro Improvement Association (UNIA), founded
the Commandment Keepers Congregation in Harlem in 1919. Matthew established close
ties with Ethiopian Jewry, adopted standard Ashkenazi and Sephardi rabbinic liturgy,
and trained dozens of rabbis who established synagogal communities across the United
States and the Caribbean.

In the late 1960s, Ben Ammi Ben-Israel (1939–2014) led the African Hebrew Israelites
of Jerusalem from Chicago to Liberia and subsequently to Israel, establishing a
flourishing, permanent communal settlement in the Negev desert city of Dimona. Concurrently,
more radical, confrontational factions emerged from the Israeli School of Universal
Practical Knowledge (ISUPK), established in New York by Abba Bivens in 1969, giving
rise to numerous independent street-preaching camps.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

The foundational theological premise uniting Hebrew Israelites is the claim that
people of Black African descent are the authentic physical descendants of the
biblical patriarchs (Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob). Adherents point to Deuteronomy 28:68
("And the Lord shall bring thee into Egypt again with ships...") as prophetic proof
that the Transatlantic Slave Trade was divine punishment for Israel's historical
disobedience to the Torah covenants.

Scholars emphasize that the movement is not a monolithic organization but a broad,
highly varied theological continuum. At one end of the spectrum are mainstream,
conciliatory congregations like the Commandment Keepers and the International Israelite
Board of Rabbis, which practice normative Rabbinic Judaism, maintain warm relations
with Jewish communities, and emphasize peaceful civic integration and Hebrew
scholarship. The African Hebrew Israelites of Dimona practice strict veganism,
polygyny, and holistic health, achieving permanent residency in Israel.

At the other end of the spectrum are militant 1 West camps (such as ISUPK and the
Israelite Church of God in Jesus Christ), known for public street confrontations in
major American cities, apocalyptic rhetoric, and anti-white and anti-Semitic doctrines
that designate white people as descendants of Esau/Edom doomed to subjugation. A small
number of these radical fringe groups have been designated hate groups by civil rights
monitoring organizations. Overall, the Hebrew Israelite movement represents a complex
expression of African American religious self-definition, historical reinterpretation,
and search for sovereign dignity beyond the confines of transatlantic slavery and
colonial Christianity.
