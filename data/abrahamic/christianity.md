# Christianity

## Historical genesis

Christianity began within the diverse Judaism of first-century Roman Judea. Jesus's followers interpreted his teaching, execution, and experiences of his resurrection through Israel's scriptures and Jewish expectations about messiah, kingdom, covenant, and the restoration of creation. Networks associated with Paul and other missionaries admitted non-Jews without requiring full Torah observance, helping form communities throughout the eastern Mediterranean.

Separation from Judaism was gradual, uneven, and regionally varied. The destruction of Jerusalem in 70 CE, developing Christian claims about Jesus, changing communal institutions, and later imperial law all contributed. By the fourth century, bishops and councils worked to define doctrine amid disputes about Christ, God, scripture, and authority. Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, Roman Catholic, Protestant, restorationist, and independent churches subsequently produced a plural family rather than one uniform institution.

## Ideas and practice

Most Christian traditions confess one God, understand Jesus as Christ and Lord, practice baptism and Eucharist, and receive the Bible as scripture. The doctrines of Trinity and incarnation emerged through long exegetical and philosophical controversies. Salvation may be described as forgiveness, liberation, participation in divine life, moral transformation, or victory over death, with different churches ordering those images differently.

## Comparative notes

“Gnosticism” is a modern umbrella term for several ancient movements, not a single church that cleanly split from Christianity. Similarity alone does not establish transmission.
