# Kharijites

## Historical context

The Kharijite movement emerged during the First Fitna (656–661 CE), the first civil war of early Islam. At the Battle of Siffin (657 CE) between Caliph Ali ibn Abi Talib and Mu'awiya ibn Abi Sufyan, the governor of Syria, Ali agreed to submit the dispute over the murder of the third caliph, Uthman, to human arbitration. A dedicated faction of Ali's army protested with the slogan *la hukma illa lillah* ("Judgment belongs to God alone"), arguing that divine law had already established the legitimacy of the caliph and that submitting divine commands to human negotiation constituted apostasy.

Withdrawing from Ali's camp to the village of Harura near Kufa (earning them the early name *Haruriyya*), the dissidents elected their own leader. After negotiations failed, Ali clashed militarily with them at the Battle of Nahrawan (658 CE), inflicting heavy casualties. In retaliation, a Kharijite named Abd al-Rahman ibn Muljam assassinated Ali in Kufa in 661 CE. Throughout the Umayyad and early Abbasid periods, Kharijite groups led recurring, fierce revolts across Mesopotamia, Persia, Arabia, and North Africa. The movement fragmented into competing factions of varying severity—most violently the Azariqa, alongside the Najdat, the Sufriyya, and the moderate Ibadiyya. By approximately 900 CE, the militant Kharijite trunk had been politically suppressed and extinguished across the central Islamic lands.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Kharijite theology introduced the first major doctrinal debates in Islamic history regarding the definition of a believer (*mu'min*), sin, and political authority. In contrast to the proto-Sunni and Murji'ite positions that reserved the judgment of sinners to God and maintained that a sinning Muslim remained within the community of believers, Kharijites held that major sins immediately nullified faith, classifying the unrepentant sinner as an apostate (*kafir*).

Politically, the Kharijites rejected the prevailing Arab consensus that the caliphate was restricted to the Prophet's tribe of Quraysh. They championed a radically egalitarian doctrine of leadership: any morally upright Muslim, even an enslaved person or non-Arab, was eligible to be elected caliph, and if that ruler committed injustice or major sin, the community was religiously obligated to depose or execute them.

In later Islamic heresiography and modern political discourse, the term "Khariji" or "Khawarij" became a polemical epithet used by orthodox Sunni and Shi'i authorities to denounce violent contemporary sectarian movements and takfiri groups. Modern groups so labelled are not historical continuations of the 7th-century movement. Furthermore, the surviving Ibadi tradition of Oman and North Africa originated from moderate Basran dissenters who broke with the violent extremism of the Azariqa; modern Ibadis explicitly reject the designation "Kharijite" as a descriptor of their tradition.
