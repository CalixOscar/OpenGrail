# Bektashism

## Historical context

Bektashism traces its spiritual lineage to Haji Bektash Veli (c. 1209–1271 CE), a Khorasani dervish and mystic who migrated to central Anatolia during the Mongol invasions and settled in Sulucakarahöyük (modern Hacıbektaş, Cappadocia). The movement was formally organized into an institutionalized dervish order (*tariqa*) by Balım Sultan (d. 1516), revered within the tradition as the *Pîr-i Sânî* (Second Master).

During the Ottoman Empire, Bektashism achieved immense institutional and political influence due to its official affiliation with the elite Janissary infantry (*Yeniçeri*), who looked to Haji Bektash as their patron saint. In 1826, Sultan Mahmud II abolished the Janissary corps in the Auspicious Incident and simultaneously suppressed the Bektashi order, executing its leadership and transferring its properties to the orthodox Naqshbandi tariqa. Despite persecution, the order survived in Anatolia and flourished throughout the Ottoman Balkans, especially in Albania, Kosovo, and Macedonia.

Following the establishment of the Turkish Republic and the 1925 ban on all dervish lodges and monasteries, the World Headquarters (*Kryegjyshata Botërore Bektashiane*) relocated to Tirana, Albania in 1930. The order endured severe suppression under the Enver Hoxha communist regime (which banned all religion in Albania from 1967 to 1990) before re-emerging in 1991. Today, Bektashism is a living, officially recognized religious community centered in Tirana with communities across the Balkans and the global diaspora.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Bektashi theology combines the non-dualist Sufi metaphysics of *Wahdat al-Wujud* (Unity of Being) with intense devotion to Ali ibn Abi Talib, his wife Fatima, and the Twelve Imams of the Ahl al-Bayt. Bektashis interpret Islamic legal precepts through an esoteric (*batini*) lens, giving primacy to the four ascending spiritual stages: *Şeriat* (religious law), *Tarikat* (the mystical path), *Marifet* (gnosis), and *Hakikat* (ultimate truth).

Communal worship centers on the *cem* ceremony in a lodge (*tekke*), where men and women participate together in prayer, sacred poetry (*nefes*), and the ritual spiritual dance (*semah*). The relationship between Bektashism and Hurufism is a subject of scholarly study: following the execution of Fazlallah Astarabadi in 1394, Hurufi refugees introduced cabalistic letter mysticism into Bektashi poetry and calligraphic iconography. However, mainstream academic consensus regards this influence as an aesthetic and esoteric borrowing rather than a full adoption of Hurufi incarnationist theology, which is why the relationship is classified under minority scholarly certainty.
