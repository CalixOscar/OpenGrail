# Nuqtavi Movement

## Historical context

The Nuqtavi movement (*Nuqtaviyya*, from the Arabic *nuqta*, meaning "point" or "dot") was founded in Persia around 1397 CE by Mahmud Pasikhani (d. 1427/28 CE), a native of Gilan. Mahmud was initially a close student of the Hurufi founder Fazlallah Astarabadi, but his insistence on his own spiritual pre-eminence led Fazlallah to banish him from the community, branding him *Mahmud-i Mardud* ("Mahmud the Rejected"). In response, Mahmud established a separate movement based in Anjedan.

During the 16th century, Nuqtavism spread widely across Iran as an underground intellectual, artistic, and popular counter-cultural current. Its adherents predicted that the impending Islamic millennium (1000 AH / 1591–1592 CE) would witness the formal termination of the Islamic dispensation and the public enthronement of the Nuqtavi religion. The movement attracted prominent poets, calligraphers, and courtiers during the reigns of the Safavid monarchs Shah Tahmasp I and Shah Abbas I.

In 1593 CE, alarmed by the apocalyptic expectations of the Nuqtavis and perceiving a direct political threat to his dynasty, Shah Abbas I launched a systematic purge across Isfahan, Kashan, and Qazvin, executing major leaders such as Darvish Khusraw and Mir Sayyid Ahmad Kashi. Surviving Nuqtavi intellectuals fled east to the Mughal Empire, where they found receptive patronage in the eclectic, pluralistic court of Emperor Akbar and scholars like Abu'l-Fazl. By the middle of the 17th century (c. 1650 CE), subsequent Safavid repression and the restoration of orthodox Mughal patronage under Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb led to the complete historical disappearance of the Nuqtavis.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Nuqtavi doctrine shifted the cabalistic focus of Hurufism from speech and celestial letters to an elemental, materialist cosmology. Mahmud Pasikhani designated the primordial point (*nuqta*) as the atom of earth (*khak*), from which all physical and spiritual existence sequentially evolved through mineral, vegetable, animal, and human stages.

The Nuqtavis rejected conventional Abrahamic eschatology—including creation *ex nihilo*, divine judgment, physical resurrection, and transcendent heaven and hell. Instead, they posited an infinite, uncreated universe characterized by cyclical reincarnation (*tanasukh* or *buruz*), wherein human spiritual attainment determines the refinement of matter in subsequent rebirths. Mahmud also developed a cultural-linguistic philosophy holding that the Arabic letter-cycle represented the era of divine speech, whereas the Persian letter-cycle (which contains dotted letters) represented the era of the primordial point and Persian intellectual supremacy.
