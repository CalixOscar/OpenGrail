# Hurufism

## Historical context

Hurufism (*Hurufiyya*, from the Arabic *huruf*, meaning "letters") was founded in the late 14th century by the Persian mystic and visionary Fazlallah Astarabadi (also known as Fazlullah Naimi, 1339/40–1394 CE). Following a period of rigorous asceticism and mystical travels across Khurasan, Iraq, and Azerbaijan, Fazlallah experienced a major spiritual illumination in Tabriz around 1386 CE (788 AH), which he interpreted as the revelation of the inner, esoteric significance of the alphabet and the final unveiling of divine secrets.

Fazlallah gathered a devoted network of disciples across Persia, Azerbaijan, and Anatolia. His millenarian claims, messianic self-identification, and growing social influence alarmed the Timurid ruler Timur (Tamerlane) and orthodox Muslim jurists. In 1394 CE, under the orders of Timur's son Miran Shah, Fazlallah was captured, imprisoned, and executed at the fortress of Alinja near Nakhchivan.

Following their founder's martyrdom, Hurufi missionaries dispersed widely to escape persecution. The eminent Azerbaijani Hurufi poet Imadaddin Nesîmî spread the doctrine across Anatolia and Syria before being executed for heresy in Aleppo in 1417. In the Ottoman realm, Hurufis briefly secured access to the court of the young Sultan Mehmed II in Edirne before being expelled and executed in the 1440s through the intervention of the Grand Mufti Fahreddin Acemi. The movement continued underground, deeply influencing the poetry, talismans, and doctrines of various dervish orders—most notably the Bektashis—until fading as an independent organized movement around 1600 CE.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Hurufi theology represented a radical synthesis of Sufi metaphysics, cabalistic letter mysticism (*ilm al-huruf*), and cyclical millenarianism. Fazlallah divided cosmic history into three successive cycles: the Cycle of Prophecy (*nubuwwa*), which commenced with Adam and concluded with Muhammad (symbolized by the 28 letters of the Arabic alphabet); the Cycle of Sainthood (*walaya*), spanning the era of the Shi'i Imams from Ali to Hasan al-Askari; and the Cycle of Divine Manifestation (*uluhiyya* or *zuhur*), initiated by Fazlallah himself through the 32 letters of the Persian-augmented alphabet.

Central to Hurufi thought was the doctrine that the human form, particularly the human face, constitutes the ultimate mirror of the divine word. The lines, facial features, and hair of the human head were interpreted as physical inscriptions of the 28 and 32 cosmic letters. Mainstream Sunni and Twelver Shi'i scholars severely condemned Hurufism for its incarnationist (*hulul*) tendencies, its replacement of standard Islamic ritual commandments with esoteric letter contemplation, and its claims of post-Qur'anic revelation.
