# Ismailism

## Historical context

Ismaili communities emerged from disagreement over succession after Ja'far al-Sadiq in the eighth century. A far-reaching missionary network supported the Fatimid caliphate, founded in North Africa in 909 and later centered in Cairo. Further succession disputes produced Nizari and Tayyibi Musta'li branches, each developing diasporic institutions and distinct lines of authority.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Ismaili thought emphasizes the Imam's authoritative guidance and layered interpretation that relates outward practice to inward meaning. Fatimid scholars contributed to philosophy, law, administration, and learning; historical caricatures of secretive “Assassins” distort Nizari life. Ismailism is internally diverse: Nizaris recognize a present living Imam, while Tayyibi communities follow a line of concealed Imams represented through missionary leadership. It shares Shi'a devotion to the Prophet's family without following the Twelver succession.
