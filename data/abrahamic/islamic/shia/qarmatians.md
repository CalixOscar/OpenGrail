# Qarmatians

## Historical context

The Qarmatians originated in the late 9th century in the Sawad (rural plains) of Kufa under the leadership of the Ismaili missionary (*da'i*) Hamdan Qarmat. When the central Ismaili leadership in Salamiyya, led by Abdallah al-Mahdi Billah (the future founder of the Fatimid Caliphate), announced in 899 CE that the movement's leaders were themselves living imams rather than merely trustees awaiting the hidden seventh imam Muhammad ibn Isma'il, a profound schism erupted. Hamdan Qarmat and his regional deputies severed ties with Salamiyya, maintaining the original messianic doctrine.

In eastern Arabia (the historic province of Bahrayn, centered on al-Hasa and Qatif), Abu Sa'id al-Hasan al-Jannabi established a powerful Qarmatian state in 899 CE. Governed by a council of elders known as the *Iqdaniyya*, this state organized a militarized, communal society. Under Abu Sa'id's son Abu Tahir al-Jannabi, the Qarmatians conducted daring raids against Abbasid armies, southern Iraqi cities, and trans-Arabian pilgrimage caravans.

In January 930 CE, Abu Tahir led a raid on Mecca during the Hajj pilgrimage. Qarmatian forces killed pilgrims within the sacred precinct, desecrated the Well of Zamzam with corpses, and pried the Black Stone (*al-Hajar al-Aswad*) from the corner of the Kaaba, transporting it to their stronghold in al-Hasa. The Black Stone remained held in eastern Arabia for over twenty-one years until 952 CE, when it was returned to Mecca under Fatimid diplomatic pressure and the payment of a heavy ransom. The Qarmatian state subsequently suffered severe ideological crises, notably after the brief, disastrous elevation of a young Persian prisoner as the incarnate God-Mahdi in 931 CE. Gradual political fragmentation, military defeats against the Abbasids and Buyids, and local tribal rebellions culminated in the definitive destruction of the state by the Uyunid dynasty in 1077 CE.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

The Qarmatians adhered to an uncompromising, apocalyptic form of early Sevener Ismailism. They taught that world history was structured into seven great prophetic eras, with the seventh era inaugurating the rule of the *Qa'im* (Resurrector), who would reveal the inner esoteric truths (*batin*) and render obsolete the exterior ritual obligations (*zahir*) and legal codes (*sharia*) of previous dispensations.

Socially, the Qarmatian state in Bahrayn was characterized by unusual communal and egalitarian practices. Tenth- and eleventh-century visitors, such as the Persian traveler Nasir Khusraw in 1051 CE, documented a system in which agricultural production, grain mills, and public bakeries were subsidized or managed collectively by the ruling council without regular taxation on the citizens, sustained by communal treasury funds and regional tribute.
