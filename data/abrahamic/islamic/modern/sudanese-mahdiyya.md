# Sudanese Mahdiyya

## Historical context

In June 1881, on Aba Island in the White Nile, the Sudanese religious scholar and Sammaniyya Sufi shaykh Muhammad Ahmad ibn Abd Allah (1844–1885) publicly proclaimed himself to be the Expected Mahdi (*al-Mahdi al-Muntazar*), the messianic figure sent to restore justice and purify Islam. Capitalizing on widespread popular resentment against heavy Ottoman-Egyptian taxation, bureaucratic corruption, and European influence, Muhammad Ahmad rallied diverse tribal groups, clerics, and pastoralists, organizing them into a unified revolutionary community known as the *Ansar* (the Helpers).

The Mahdist forces achieved a succession of military victories against Egyptian and British-officered armies, notably annihilating the expedition of William Hicks Pasha in 1883. In January 1885, after a prolonged siege, Mahdist forces captured Khartoum, where British Major-General Charles George Gordon was killed. Muhammad Ahmad established his capital across the Nile at Omdurman, founding an independent Islamic theocracy.

Following Muhammad Ahmad's sudden death in June 1885, leadership passed to his chief lieutenant, Abdallahi ibn Muhammad (known as the *Khalifa*). The Khalifa consolidated administrative, fiscal, and military control over the Sudanese state, defending its borders against regional adversaries (including the Ethiopian Empire) and internal rebellions for over a decade. In 1898, a combined Anglo-Egyptian expeditionary force under Lord Kitchener defeated the main Mahdist army at the Battle of Omdurman. The death of the Khalifa at the Battle of Umm Diwaykarat in November 1899 brought an end to the historical Mahdist state.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

The Mahdiyya instituted radical religious and legal reforms. Claiming direct spiritual encounters and visionary communion with the Prophet Muhammad, Muhammad Ahmad abrogated the four classical Sunni legal schools (*madhhabs*), dissolved preexisting Sufi orders (*turuq*), and prohibited the study of scholastic theology (*kalam*) and classical jurisprudence (*fiqh*). The law of the state was governed exclusively by the Qur'an, authentic prophetic traditions, and the Mahdi's personal proclamations (*manshurat*).
