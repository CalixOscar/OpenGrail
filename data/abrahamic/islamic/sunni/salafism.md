# Salafism

## Historical context

Modern Salafism draws on earlier Sunni traditionalism and reform associated especially with Ibn Taymiyya and the eighteenth-century Najdi movement, while the label also acquired new meanings in nineteenth- and twentieth-century debates. Print, universities, migration, and Gulf patronage helped create transnational networks. Salafis differ over political participation, obedience to rulers, and militancy.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Salafis advocate direct conformity to Qur'an and authenticated sunna as understood by the pious ancestors, criticize many later theological speculations, and often reject devotional innovation around saints. The movement includes politically quietist, activist, and jihadist tendencies; equating every Salafi with violent extremism is inaccurate. Its theology often overlaps Hanbali-Athari sources, but Salafism is not simply the Hanbali legal school and may challenge strict school adherence.
