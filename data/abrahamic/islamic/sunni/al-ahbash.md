# Al-Ahbash

## Historical context

Al-Ahbash ("The Ethiopians") takes its popular designation from Shaykh Abdullah al-Harari al-Habashi (1910–2008), an Islamic scholar born in Harar, Ethiopia. After studying under leading scholars in Ethiopia, Mecca, Medina, and Damascus, Shaykh al-Harari settled in Beirut, Lebanon in 1950, where he established a reputation as a teacher of traditional Sunni jurisprudence and hadith.

In 1983, Shaykh al-Harari assumed the religious guidance of the Association of Islamic Charitable Projects (AICP; *Jam'iyyat al-Mashari' al-Khayriyya al-Islamiyya*), a civic and charitable organization originally founded in Beirut in 1930. Under his spiritual leadership, the AICP expanded into a highly structured, international movement. It established a vast network of private elementary and secondary schools, orphanages, medical clinics, mosques, cultural institutions, and media operations across Lebanon, subsequently opening branches in Europe, North America, Australia, and the Middle East.

Following the assassination of AICP President Shaykh Nizar Halabi by Islamist militants in Beirut in 1995, the movement continued to participate actively in Lebanese municipal and parliamentary elections, aligning with secular and pluralist factions within Lebanon's multi-confessional system.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Al-Ahbash positions itself as a champion of classical Sunni orthodoxy against the modern currents of Salafism/Wahhabism and political Islamism (such as the Muslim Brotherhood and Sayyid Qutb's ideology). Theologically, the movement is uncompromisingly Ash'ari and Maturidi, emphasizing *tanzih*—the absolute transcendence of God beyond time, space, direction, and bodily form—and condemning anthropomorphic interpretations of divine attributes. In legal matters, it adheres predominantly to the Shafi'i school, while actively embracing Sufi devotional practices and saint veneration (*ziyara*).

Socially, the AICP promotes integration within pluralistic societies. The movement encourages female education and professional employment, permits Western attire, and defends civil secular governance in religiously diverse states. Because of its vigorous public polemics and counter-takfir against prominent medieval figures like Ibn Taymiyyah and contemporary Salafi authorities, Al-Ahbash has faced widespread sectarian opposition and hostility from rival Islamist movements.
