# Orthodox Judaism

## Historical context

“Orthodox” became a denominational label in nineteenth-century Europe as Jewish emancipation, Reform, secularization, and new educational institutions transformed communal life. It encompasses Haredi, Hasidic, Modern Orthodox, and other communities rather than one central organization. Migration, the Holocaust, Zionism, the State of Israel, and expanding educational networks reshaped its modern centers.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Orthodox communities affirm halakhah as binding and ground it in written and oral Torah, while differing over legitimate legal change, secular learning, gender, politics, and engagement with wider culture. Rabbinic decisors interpret inherited sources for new conditions; Orthodoxy is therefore not simply frozen premodern practice. It shares the rabbinic canon with Conservative and Reform Judaism but assigns different weight to traditional authority and historical criticism.
