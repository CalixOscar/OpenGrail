# Dönmeh

## Historical context

The Dönmeh (from the Ottoman Turkish *dönmek*, meaning "to turn" or "to convert"; self-designated in Hebrew as *Ma'aminim*, "the Believers") originated in Ottoman Salonica (Thessaloniki) in the late seventeenth century. Following the death of Shabbetai Tzvi in 1676, several hundred families of his followers in Salonica, led by Shabbetai's brother-in-law Jacob Querido, underwent formal conversion to Islam in 1683 while secretly preserving their Sabbatean faith and communal identity.

Salonica served as the world center of the Dönmeh for over two centuries, where they developed a sophisticated socio-religious infrastructure. The community divided into three main sub-sects: the *İzmirli* (Kapancı), adhering to the original leadership line; the *Yakubi*, followers of Jacob Querido; and the *Karakash* (Konyoso), followers of Baruchiah Russo (Osman Baba), who introduced more radical antinomian teachings. Members played prominent roles in the economic modernization, educational reform, and intellectual life of late Ottoman Salonica.

Following the 1923 population exchange between Greece and Turkey, the Dönmeh, classified legally as Muslims, were relocated to Istanbul and other Turkish cities. In the modern Turkish Republic, the community largely integrated into secular urban society, though family lineages and cultural memories persist into the twenty-first century.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

The Dönmeh developed a unique dual religious posture. Unlike Conversos in Christian Iberia who were forced into baptism, the Dönmeh embraced outward conversion as an active theological imperative instituted by their messiah. Their ethical and communal conduct was governed by the *Eighteen Commandments of Shabbetai Tzvi*, which mandated outward conformity to Islamic law, complete concealment of their beliefs from outsiders (both Jews and non-Sabbatean Muslims), strict internal endogamy, and avoidance of intermarriage with outside groups.

Liturgy was conducted privately in Judeo-Spanish (Ladino) and Hebrew, preserving hymns (*zemirot*) and prayers dedicated to Shabbetai Tzvi. Simultaneously, the Dönmeh maintained close affinities with Ottoman Sufism, particularly the heterodox Bektashi and Mevlevi orders. Many Dönmeh members affiliated with Sufi tekkes (lodges), finding in Sufi esotericism, ecstatic devotion, and allegorical interpretations of Islamic law a natural complement to their kabbalistic convictions.

Modern historical scholarship, led by Gershom Scholem, Marc David Baer, and Cengiz Şişman, has documented the internal literary and ritual life of the Dönmeh from primary archival manuscripts, while analyzing the complex ways in which Dönmeh identity intersected with late Ottoman modernization and modern Turkish secularism.
