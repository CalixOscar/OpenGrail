# Frankism

## Historical context

Frankism was an extreme messianic and antinomian movement founded in mid-eighteenth-century Podolia (then part of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth) by Jacob Frank (born Jakub Lejbowicz, 1726–1791). Frank had spent his youth in Ottoman Walachia and the Balkans, where he was initiated into radical Sabbatean circles affiliated with the Dönmeh. Returning to Podolia in 1755, Frank organized a clandestine sect that claimed to transcend traditional Jewish law.

In January 1756, a secret Frankist gathering in Lanckorona was discovered by local Jews, triggering an investigation and formal cherem (rabbinic excommunication) against the group by the rabbinical authorities of Brody. Seeking protection from Catholic authorities, the Frankists declared themselves "Anti-Talmudists" or "Zoharists," asserting that they accepted the Kabbalistic Zohar while rejecting the Talmud as a corrupt human invention. Supported by Bishop Dembowski of Kamieniec-Podolski, the Frankists participated in public disputations with rabbinic representatives in 1757 and 1759.

In 1759, Frank led thousands of his followers into mass baptism into the Roman Catholic Church in Lwów and Warsaw, with King Augustus III acting as Frank's godfather. However, the Polish ecclesiastical authorities soon suspected that the Frankists' conversion was insincere and that they continued to venerate Frank as a living deity. In 1760, Frank was arrested and imprisoned in the fortress monastery of Częstochowa for thirteen years. Released during the Russian occupation in 1772, Frank established lavish aristocratic courts in Brno (Moravia) and later in Offenbach am Main (Germany), where he lived as the "Baron of Offenbach." Following his death in 1791 and the leadership of his daughter Eve Frank (1754–1816), the movement declined and dissolved into the Polish Catholic and assimilated European nobility by approximately 1820.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Frankism pushed the antinomian premises of Sabbateanism to their most radical and nihilistic conclusions. While Shabbetai Tzvi and Nathan of Gaza understood transgression as a tragic necessity to rescue trapped divine sparks, Frank taught that all traditional religions, moral codes, and sacred scriptures were enslaving structures of the cosmic archons (*The Great Brothers*). To reach the true, hidden "Living God" (*El Chai*), believers were required to follow Frank into the "burden of silence" and the "way to Esau"—namely, outward embrace of Christianity combined with absolute inward detachment.

Frank gathered his inner circle into military-like companies, practiced ritualized sexual libertinism during esoteric night rituals, and composed *Divrei ha-Adon* (*The Words of the Lord*), a collection of parabolic, visionary, and often coarse homilies recorded in Polish and Yiddish. Frank portrayed salvation as a perilous journey into total lawlessness, teaching that "I have come to bring not life, but destruction."

In historical sociology and Jewish studies, notably through the work of Pawel Maciejko and Gershom Scholem, Frankism is recognized as a complex syncretic phenomenon. The collective baptism of several thousand Polish Jews altered the social landscape of late eighteenth-century Poland, with Frankist descendants contributing significantly to the Polish legal, cultural, and political elite of the nineteenth century.
