# Gnostic Traditions

## Historical genesis

“Gnosticism” is a retrospective category applied to a range of movements flourishing mainly in the second and third centuries CE. Important evidence comes from critics such as Irenaeus as well as manuscripts found near Nag Hammadi in 1945. Sethian, Valentinian, Basilidean, and other materials differ substantially, and some scholars prefer to discuss each network without treating them as branches of a single religion.

Many sources reinterpret Jewish creation narratives and Christian claims about Jesus through complex myth. A transcendent divine fullness may stand beyond a flawed or ignorant creator; a revealer awakens people to their origin beyond the visible cosmos. Yet not every text is anti-cosmic or “dualist,” and knowledge (*gnōsis*) was important in many ancient schools that are not conventionally called Gnostic.

## Ideas and practice

Surviving texts describe baptismal rites, anointing, visionary ascent, ethical instruction, and sacramental meals. Valentinian teachers could participate in wider Christian congregations while cultivating additional interpretations. The familiar picture of uniformly elitist groups rejecting bodily life comes largely from hostile reports and must be checked against their own varied writings.

## Comparative notes

Jewish scripture, Christian proclamation, Middle Platonism, and Egyptian settings all contributed to the environments in which these traditions formed. Resemblances between Gnostic cosmic opposition and Zoroastrian dualism have encouraged comparison, but direct descent is difficult to demonstrate. The graph therefore separates historically strong Jewish and Christian connections from a lower-certainty Zoroastrian parallel.
