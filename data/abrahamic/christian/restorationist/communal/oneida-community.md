# Oneida Community

## Historical context

The Oneida Community was an intentional perfectionist Christian communal society
established in 1848 in Oneida, New York, by John Humphrey Noyes (1811–1886) and a
circle of around two hundred followers. Noyes, educated at Dartmouth, Andover, and
Yale Divinity School, experienced religious conversion during Charles Grandison
Finney's Second Great Awakening revivals. In 1834, Noyes declared that he had attained
a state of absolute sinlessness (*Perfectionism*), a theological stance that led to the
revocation of his preaching license. Noyes first organized a communal association in
Putney, Vermont, in the early 1840s before local legal pressure and hostility over his
unorthodox marital theories prompted the community's move to upstate New York.

At Oneida, the community constructed the sprawling Oneida Mansion House and developed
a remarkably prosperous diversified economy, manufacturing animal traps, silk thread,
canned fruits, and eventually high-quality silver-plated flatware. Members worked in
rotating communal departments with egalitarian gender roles, women wearing shortened
bloomer dresses and short hair for practical labor. Despite economic prosperity,
growing internal conflict over the community's stirpiculture eugenic breeding program
(introduced in 1869) and Noyes's aging leadership, coupled with outside legal threats
of prosecution for statutory rape, brought about the community's unraveling. Noyes fled
to Ontario, Canada, in 1879 to avoid arrest. In 1880, the community voted to abandon
complex marriage, and on January 1, 1881, it formally dissolved its communal property,
reorganizing as the joint-stock company Oneida Community, Limited (later Oneida
Silversmiths).

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Oneida theology was grounded in Noyes's distinctive eschatology and social
philosophy, which he termed "Bible Communism." Rejecting traditional Christian
futurist millenarianism, Noyes taught that the Second Coming of Christ had already
taken place in 70 CE at the fall of the Temple in Jerusalem. Consequently, the apostolic
age of perfection was available to contemporary believers who fully surrendered
selfishness and yielded to the Holy Spirit. Noyes argued that private property and
exclusive marriage were twin forms of selfish possessiveness that had no place in the
kingdom of heaven.

Under the system of "complex marriage," every adult member was considered married to
every other member of the opposite sex, though sexual unions required mutual consent
and the approval of community elders. To decouple sexual intimacy from unwanted
pregnancy, Noyes mandated "male continence" (coitus reservatus), in which men avoided
ejaculation during intercourse; this served both as an effective method of birth
control and as a spiritual discipline of self-mastery. The community also practiced
"mutual criticism," structured assemblies where a member sat in silence while peers
frankly evaluated their character flaws, selfish tendencies, and spiritual progress.
Unlike celibate communal groups like the Shakers, Oneida sought to sanctify and
collectivize sexuality, emotional life, and industrial enterprise within a single
perfectionist social order.
