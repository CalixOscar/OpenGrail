# Peace Mission Movement

## Historical context

The International Peace Mission Movement was founded by the charismatic African
American spiritual leader Father Divine (born George Baker c. 1876–1965). After
itinerant preaching in the American South and association with early Holiness and
Pentecostal preachers such as Samuel Morris (Father Jehovia) and St. John Divine Bishop
in the 1890s and 1900s, Father Divine settled in Sayville, Long Island, New York, in
1919 with his first wife, Peninniah (Sister Devout). There they established a communal
household that welcomed people of all races, providing lodging and sumptuous free
banquets. As word of his miraculous provision spread, thousands of pilgrims flocked to
Sayville.

In 1931, local white residents complained of noise and racial mixing, leading to Father
Divine's arrest for maintaining a public nuisance. When the presiding trial judge,
Lewis J. Smith, died of a sudden heart attack days after sentencing Divine to a year in
prison, Divine's famous remark—"I hated to do it"—cemented his followers' conviction of
his divine omnipotence. Moving his headquarters to Harlem, New York, in 1932, the
Peace Mission expanded dramatically during the Great Depression. The movement
operated dozens of "Peace Mission extensions," hotels, bakeries, grocery stores, and
restaurants serving low-cost and free meals to tens of thousands across Harlem,
Philadelphia, and other urban centers. In 1946, following Peninniah's death, Father
Divine married Edna Rose Ritchings, a young Canadian woman known as Mother Divine (the
"Sweet Angel"), who led the movement from the Woodmont estate near Philadelphia after
Father Divine's death in 1965.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

The central theological pillar of the Peace Mission Movement is the belief that
Father Divine was God Almighty manifested in a human body. Blending elements of
Holiness Christian perfectionism, Pentecostal pneumatic intensity, and New Thought
metaphysics regarding the power of positive thinking, Father Divine taught that God's
presence could transform physical reality, disease, poverty, and racial oppression
into immediate abundance and peace.

The movement was notable for its radical commitment to racial equality at the height
of the Jim Crow era. Father Divine taught that race was a mental delusion and
strictly prohibited racial terms among his followers, referring to individuals merely
as "so-called Negroes" or "so-called whites." Followers adopted "angelic names" (such
as Joy Love, Faithful Mary, or Beautiful Truth) upon joining, surrendered private
property, and lived in gender-segregated communal homes where strict celibacy was
practiced. Economically, the Peace Mission championed self-sufficiency, honest cash
transactions, and absolute refusal of debt, charity, welfare, or life insurance. In
1936, the movement promulgated the "Righteous Government Platform," advocating
universal civil rights, anti-lynching legislation, economic justice, and international
peace.
