# Shakers

## Historical context

The Shakers, formally known as the United Society of Believers in Christ's Second
Appearing, originated in the mid-eighteenth century in Bolton and Manchester, England.
The movement arose within a group of dissenting Quakers led by James and Jane Wardley,
who were influenced by the charismatic prophesying and physical agitation of the
exiled French Camisards (*French Prophets*). In 1758, Ann Lee (1736–1784), a Manchester
blacksmith's daughter, joined the group. Following repeated imprisonments for Sabbath
disturbances and profound visionary experiences while incarcerated in 1770, Lee was
recognized by the believers as "Mother Ann," the spiritual mother through whom the
female aspect of the divine was revealed to humanity.

Facing intense persecution and mob violence in England, Mother Ann Lee, her husband,
and a small band of eight disciples emigrated to colonial New York in 1774, settling
at Watervliet (Niskayuna) near Albany. Following the Second Great Awakening revival
sweeps in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, the Shaker movement
expanded rapidly across New York, New England, Ohio, Kentucky, and Indiana. Under the
organizational leadership of Joseph Meacham and Lucy Wright, the Shakers established
tightly coordinated communal villages governed by equal dual male and female
ministries. At its nineteenth-century peak, the Society comprised more than 6,000
believers across eighteen major communities. Although membership declined steeply in
the twentieth century, a small active remnant community persists at Sabbathday Lake
Shaker Village in Maine.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Shaker theology presents a radical development of restorationist and perfectionist
Christianity. God is understood as a dual being having both male and female essences:
the male principle was revealed in Jesus of Nazareth, while the female principle was
manifested in Mother Ann Lee. Because the kingdom of heaven was inaugurated in the
present world through Christ's second appearance, believers were called to live the
angelic life on earth, which required the absolute renunciation of sexual relations
and biological family structures in favor of universal spiritual brotherhood and
sisterhood.

Communal life was organized around the "order of the gospel," uniting rigorous
practical discipline with spiritual ecumenism. The Shakers held all property in
common, rejected private wealth, refused military service and state oaths, and
practiced full gender equality in spiritual and administrative governance more than a
century before women attained civil suffrage. Their ecstatic worship featured
choreographed marches, ring dances, rhythmic shaking, and spirit-inspired gift songs
and drawings (such as the Era of Manifestations visions of 1837–1847). Shaker design,
architecture, and agriculture gained renowned historical acclaim for functional
perfection, labor-saving innovation, and exquisite minimalist craftsmanship, guided
by Mother Ann's maxim: "Hands to work and hearts to God."
