# Bogomilism

## Historical context

The movement is named for the priest Bogomil, active in Bulgaria during the reign of
Peter I in the mid tenth century, and is first described at length in the polemical
sermon of Cosmas the Priest. It spread into Byzantine territory, where Alexios I had
its leader Basil the Physician burned around 1100, and into Bosnia and Dalmatia. The
extent to which the medieval Bosnian Church was Bogomil in doctrine, rather than a
schismatic but broadly orthodox local church, is disputed and rests on limited
sources. Ottoman conquest and conversion absorbed the remaining communities by the
later fifteenth century.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Bogomil dualism was normally moderate rather than absolute: Satanael was a creature
who fell, not a co-eternal principle. The social edge of the movement was as
prominent as the theological one in hostile accounts, which describe adherents
refusing labour service and reviling the wealthy, and this may reflect the concerns of
the writers as much as the movement.

Bogomilism supplies the strongest case for a transmission line running from Armenian
and Anatolian dualism through the Balkans into Western Europe, but each link is
argued from fragmentary evidence. Claims that Bosnian stećci tombstones encode
Bogomil iconography are not supported by current archaeology and should be treated as
a nineteenth-century romantic construction.
