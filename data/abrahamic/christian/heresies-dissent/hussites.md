# Hussites

## Historical context

The burning of Jan Hus at the Council of Constance in July 1415, in breach of the
safe conduct granted by Sigismund, turned a university reform party into a national
movement. The defenestration of Prague in 1419 opened the Hussite Wars, in which
Bohemian forces under Jan Žižka and Prokop the Great defeated five crusades, partly
through the tactical use of war wagons. The moderate Utraquists came to terms with
the Council of Basel in the Compactata of 1436 and defeated the radical Taborites at
Lipany in 1434.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

The movement divided early. The Utraquists sought a reformed church within Catholic
order, holding to the chalice as the decisive point; the Taborites, based on their
fortified town in southern Bohemia, rejected purgatory, the veneration of saints, and
most of the sacramental apparatus, and for a period expected an imminent millennium.
A third strand, the Unity of the Brethren founded in 1457, developed into a pacifist
church and is a direct ancestor of the Moravian Church, so the tradition is continuous
rather than extinct.

Hussitism is conventionally described as a pre-Reformation movement, and the Bohemian
precedent was explicitly invoked at the 1519 Leipzig disputation, where Luther
acknowledged holding positions for which Hus had been condemned.
