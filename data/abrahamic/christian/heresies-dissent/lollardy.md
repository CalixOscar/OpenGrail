# Lollardy

## Historical context

Lollardy grew from the Oxford teaching of John Wycliffe in the 1370s and 1380s and
found early support among gentry and household knights. The Blackfriars council of
1382 condemned Wycliffe's eucharistic teaching, and after the failed rising of Sir
John Oldcastle in 1414 the movement lost its protectors and became a clandestine
network among artisans and traders in London, the Chilterns, Bristol, Coventry, and
Kent. Legislation of 1401 made obstinate heresy a capital offence in England, and
Arundel's Constitutions of 1409 banned unlicensed vernacular Scripture.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

The movement had no central organisation and considerable doctrinal variety; the
common threads were vernacular Bible reading, hostility to images and pilgrimage, and
denial of transubstantiation. Its literature, including the two versions of the
Wycliffite Bible, was copied and read well into the sixteenth century.

Historians disagree about continuity with the English Reformation. Surviving Lollard
communities in the 1520s demonstrably bought Tyndale's New Testament and supplied
early evangelical congregations, but whether Lollardy prepared the ground for the
Reformation or merely merged into it once it arrived remains an open question. The
early spread of Wycliffite texts to Prague, through the dynastic and university links
formed by the marriage of Anne of Bohemia to Richard II, is far better attested.
