# Catharism

## Historical context

Catharism appears in the documentary record in the Rhineland and Languedoc in the
1140s and became the dominant religious alternative across much of Occitania within
two generations, protected in practice by a fragmented and often sympathetic
nobility. The Albigensian Crusade, launched in 1209, broke that protection over two
decades of warfare, and the inquisitorial tribunals that followed dismantled the
surviving networks parish by parish. The last known perfectus in Languedoc, Guillaume
Bélibaste, was burned in 1321, and the Italian communities did not survive the
fourteenth century.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Cathar theology existed in moderate and absolute forms: the moderate position treated
the evil principle as a fallen creature, while the absolute dualism of the Italian
Book of Two Principles held two eternal opposed powers. Both rejected the Roman
sacraments, the veneration of the cross as an instrument of torture, and the
resurrection of the flesh.

The relationship to Bogomilism is the central open question. The Occitan record of the
1167 council at Saint-Félix names a Bogomil emissary, and later Latin polemicists
assumed a Balkan pedigree. Some historians read the movement instead as a largely
indigenous Western dissent that inquisitors described in imported categories, and a
strand of recent scholarship questions how far "Catharism" was a single organised
church at all rather than a construct of its persecutors. The evidence is heavily
weighted toward hostile sources, and that limitation should qualify any account of
its internal life.
