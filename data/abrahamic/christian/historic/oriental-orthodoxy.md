# Oriental Orthodoxy

## Historical context

Oriental Orthodox churches rejected or did not receive the Council of Chalcedon's 451 formula and developed enduring communities in Egypt, Syria, Armenia, Ethiopia, Eritrea, and India. They include Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, Ethiopian, Eritrean, and Malankara churches. Imperial conflict, monastic networks, translation, and life under later Muslim governments shaped their distinct histories.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

These churches describe Christ as one incarnate nature of the divine Word, a position called miaphysite rather than the polemical label “monophysite.” They preserve ancient liturgies, episcopal succession, sacraments, fasting, and strong monastic traditions. Oriental Orthodoxy is institutionally distinct from Eastern Orthodoxy. Twentieth-century dialogues found substantial agreement about Christ despite inherited terminology, but full communion has not been restored.
