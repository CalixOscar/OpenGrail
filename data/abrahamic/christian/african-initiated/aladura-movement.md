# Aladura Movement

## Historical context

The Aladura movement (from the Yoruba word *aladura*, meaning "one who prays" or
"prayer-fellowship") originated in southwestern Nigeria between 1918 and the 1930s.
The movement arose out of a profound crisis: the 1918 global influenza pandemic
devastated the Yoruba region, overwhelming colonial medical infrastructure and
prompting African Anglican Christians in Ijebu-Ode, led by Sophia Odunlami and Joseph
Sadare, to form the Precious Stone Society (or *Egbe Okuta Iyebiye*), relying
exclusively on intensive prayer and consecrated water for miraculous healing.

During the 1920s, the movement branched into several prominent independent
denominations. In 1925 in Lagos, Moses Orimolade Tunolase and Christiana Abiodun
Akinsowon co-founded the Eternal Sacred Order of the Cherubim and Seraphim Society
following Akinsowon's ecstatic angel visions. In 1930, Josiah Oshitelu founded the
Church of the Lord (Aladura) in Ogere after experiencing prophetic revelations. That
same year, Joseph Ayo Babalola led the massive Great Revival at Ilesa, raising
thousands of converts and giving rise to the Christ Apostolic Church (CAC). By the
mid-twentieth century, Aladura churches spread across Nigeria, Ghana, Sierra Leone,
and Liberia, later establishing influential international congregations throughout the
United Kingdom, Europe, and North America.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Aladura Christianity represents a powerful synthesis of biblical pneumatology,
charismatic revivalism, and traditional Yoruba spiritual worldviews. Unlike Western
missionary churches that often dismissed indigenous spiritual concerns regarding
malevolent spiritual forces, witchcraft (*aje*), and generational curses, Aladura
churches confronted these realities directly, asserting the total supremacy of Jesus
Christ and the Holy Spirit over all demonic realms.

Liturgical and ritual practices across the Aladura traditions emphasize spiritual
purity, ascetic consecration, and charismatic power. In the Cherubim and Seraphim and
Church of the Lord traditions, members worship barefoot, wearing flowing white robes
symbolizing angelic holiness and equality before God. Sanctuaries are treated as
sacred ground where incense is burned, bells are rung to announce prayers, and water
is consecrated through the recitation of specific Psalms. Services are characterized
by vigorous rhythmic drumming, Yoruba choral hymnody, clapping, ecstatic dancing, and
public testimonies of miraculous deliverance. Aladura churches pioneered female
leadership in African Christianity, recognizing women as visionary prophetesses,
evangelists, and church founders alongside male ministers.
