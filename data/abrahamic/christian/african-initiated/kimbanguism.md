# Kimbanguism

## Historical context

Kimbanguism was initiated in April 1921 in the village of Nkamba in the Lower Congo
region of the Belgian Congo (now the Democratic Republic of the Congo) by Simon
Kimbangu (1887–1951). Kimbangu, a catechist educated by the Baptist Missionary
Society, experienced a series of divine visions commanding him to preach and heal. On
April 6, 1921, Kimbangu performed his first recorded healing on a sick woman named
Kiantondo. Within weeks, tens of thousands of Congolese traveled to Nkamba to hear
his sermons, witness healings, and seek spiritual deliverance from colonial and
physical suffering.

Belgian colonial authorities and Catholic and Protestant mission hierarchies viewed
the mass movement with severe alarm, fearing anti-colonial unrest and labor strikes.
In September 1921, Kimbangu was arrested by colonial forces, court-martialed, and
sentenced to death for sedition. King Albert I of Belgium commuted his sentence to
life imprisonment; Kimbangu was exiled to Élisabethville (Lubumbashi) in Katanga,
where he remained incarcerated under harsh conditions for thirty years until his death
in 1951, never seeing his family or Nkamba again.

Despite severe colonial persecution, clandestine Kimbanguist cells survived and grew
throughout Central Africa. In 1959, on the eve of Congolese independence, the Belgian
government officially recognized the church. Led by Kimbangu's youngest son, Joseph
Diangienda Kuntima (1922–1992), the church was incorporated as the Church of Jesus
Christ on Earth by His Special Envoy Simon Kimbangu (*Église de Jésus-Christ sur la
Terre par son envoyé spécial Simon Kimbangu*, EJCSK). In 1969, the Kimbanguist Church
became the first African Independent Church admitted into the World Council of
Churches. Today, the church claims millions of adherents across the DRC, the Republic
of the Congo, Angola, and global diaspora communities.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Kimbanguism represents one of the earliest and most influential expressions of
African-initiated Christianity, pioneering the theological and cultural decolonization
of Christian practice. Theologically, Kimbanguism upholds the biblical canon and
Trinitarian theology while developing a distinctive pneumatology: Kimbangu and his
three sons (Charles Kisolokele, Salomon Dialungana, and Joseph Diangienda) are revered
within the tradition's theological evolution as manifestations of the divine
trinity and the Holy Spirit promised by Jesus in John 14.

The spiritual epicenter of the church is Nkamba, renamed *Nkamba Nouvelle Jérusalem*,
where Simon Kimbangu and his successors are interred in a massive mausoleum.
Kimbanguist liturgy combines Protestant hymnody, dynamic brass bands (*Fanfare
Kimbanguiste*), and distinctive Kikongo choral singing. The moral code is rigorously
ascetic, enjoining nonviolence, strict monogamy, hard communal labor, and total
abstinence from alcohol, tobacco, dancing, meat containing blood, and pork.
Kimbanguism played an essential role in fostering Congolese national identity, civic
self-reliance, and independent African institutional leadership.
