# Lumpa Church

## Historical context

The Lumpa Church was founded in 1953 in the Chinsali District of Northern Rhodesia
(now Zambia) by Alice Mulenga Lubusha (known as *Lenshina*, meaning "queen", c.
1920–1978). In September 1953, during a severe illness, Lenshina experienced a
near-death vision in which she was taken to heaven, met Jesus Christ, and was given
divine hymns and a mission to preach against witchcraft and cleanse her people of
sin. Initially supported by Presbyterian missionaries at the Church of Scotland's
Lubwa Mission, Lenshina soon drew thousands of pilgrims to her home village of
Kasomo, constructing a massive cathedral known as Zion.

By 1955, Lenshina broke formally with the mission churches, establishing the Lumpa
Church (*Lumpa* meaning "excelling" or "better than all others" in Bemba). The church
expanded with extraordinary speed across northern Zambia, the Copperbelt, and
neighboring Malawi, claiming over 100,000 adherents. The movement's rapid growth
depleted membership in both Catholic and Protestant mission churches and alienated
traditional Bemba chiefs.

In the early 1960s, as Northern Rhodesia moved toward independence under Kenneth
Kaunda's United National Independence Party (UNIP), tensions between Lumpa adherents
and UNIP activists escalated sharply. Lenshina demanded absolute political neutrality
and forbade her followers from joining political parties, paying party dues, or
participating in secular elections. When UNIP cadres attacked Lumpa members, the
church withdrew into fortified stockaded villages, refusing government authority. In
July and August 1964, just months before Zambian independence, military and police
forces stormed the Lumpa settlements. The resulting armed conflict claimed roughly
700 lives. The Lumpa Church was officially banned by the newly independent Zambian
government, and Lenshina was detained without trial until 1975, remaining under house
arrest until her death in Lusaka in 1978.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

The Lumpa Church represented one of the most powerful anti-witchcraft revivalist
movements in twentieth-century Central Africa. Lenshina's appeal lay in her ability to
address the existential terror of witchcraft (*buloshi*) through Christian baptism
and public purification. Thousands of converts surrendered ancestral amulets, magic
horns, and medicine pouches at Kasomo, publicly confessing their sins and finding
release from occult fear.

Lumpa worship was celebrated for its haunting, deeply emotive Bemba hymnody,
composed by Lenshina and sung without Western musical instruments. The church
promoted a strict moral code: polygamy was tolerated if established prior to baptism
but prohibited thereafter, while alcohol, smoking, traditional beer brewing, adultery,
and sorcery were absolutely forbidden. The movement's radical millenarian separatism—its
refusal to submit to the post-colonial nation-state and its insistence that
allegiance belonged exclusively to Christ—precipitated its fatal collision with the
modernizing Zambian state.
