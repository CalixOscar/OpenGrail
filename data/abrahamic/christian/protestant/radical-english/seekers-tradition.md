# Seekers

## Historical context

The term "Seeker" first appeared in England in the 1620s and gained widespread
prominence during the English Civil War in the 1640s to describe individuals and
autonomous gatherings who had separated from institutional churches. Notable
thinkers expressing Seeker convictions included John Saltmarsh, William Walwyn,
and Roger Williams (who founded Rhode Island after departing the Baptists to
become a Seeker). When George Fox and fellow Quaker publishers traveled through
northern England in 1652, entire congregations of Seekers (notably the
Westmorland Seekers meeting at Preston Patrick and Firbank Fell) embraced Fox's
message that Christ had returned to teach inwardly, merging extensively into the
early Religious Society of Friends.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Unlike other dissenting groups who claimed to have reconstructed the pure
primitive apostolic church (such as General Baptists or Fifth Monarchists),
Seekers maintained that no human effort could restore legitimate church order
without a fresh, miraculous divine commission. Consequently, they abstained from
instituting formal clergy, ordaining officers, or practicing outward sacraments.

Their practice of gathering in expectant silence to await direct divine
inspiration formed the primary devotional and institutional precedent for the
silent Quaker meeting for worship.
