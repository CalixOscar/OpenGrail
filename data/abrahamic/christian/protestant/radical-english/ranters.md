# Ranters

## Historical context

During the English Interregnum (1649–1660), following the collapse of royal and
episcopal censorship, charismatic preachers such as Abiezer Coppe, Laurence
Clarkson, and Joseph Salmon published radical pamphlets that alarmed the political
and religious establishment. In 1650, Parliament passed the Blasphemy Act
specifically targeting Ranter ideas, subjecting leaders to imprisonment and
public burning of their tracts. By the Restoration of the monarchy in 1660, the
movement had largely dispersed, with some former adherents joining the early
Quakers or Muggletonians.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Ranterism represented the most extreme antinomian edge of seventeenth-century
English radicalism. Where mainstream Puritans maintained strict moral discipline
and the authority of Scripture, Ranters argued that for those in whom God fully
dwelt, conventional moral prohibitions no longer applied and sin was an illusion
created by human judgment.

Scholarly debate persists regarding the organizational nature of the Ranters.
While contemporary hostile pamphlets and early Quaker polemics depicted an
organized, libertine sect, revisionist historians (notably J.C. Davis) argued
that the "Ranters" were largely an exaggerated polemical construct created by
authorities to discredit radical dissent. However, social historians such as
Christopher Hill emphasize that surviving authentic tracts by Coppe and Clarkson
demonstrate genuine, highly subversive theological radicalism.
