# Quakers

## Historical context

The Religious Society of Friends arose amid the religious upheaval of seventeenth-century England. George Fox and other traveling ministers rejected compulsory tithes, a professional clergy, and fixed sacramental rites, enduring imprisonment and public hostility. Quaker migration created influential communities in North America, while later evangelical, conservative, and liberal branches developed distinct theologies.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Friends emphasize the inward light or present guidance of Christ and seek decisions through corporate discernment. Some meetings worship in expectant silence without a programmed sermon; others resemble evangelical services. Testimonies concerning peace, equality, simplicity, integrity, and stewardship have inspired abolitionist and humanitarian action, though Quaker history also includes compromise with slavery and empire. Not all contemporary Friends use the same Christological language, so the movement's Protestant genealogy exceeds any single current identity.
