# Movement for the Restoration of the Ten Commandments of God

## Historical context

The Movement for the Restoration of the Ten Commandments of God (MRTCG) was
established in 1989 in southwestern Uganda. The movement originated around Credonia
Mwerinde (a former bar owner who claimed to have received apparitions of the Virgin
Mary at Nyakishenyi in 1988) and Joseph Kibwetere (a respected Catholic school
administrator and local politician). Joined by several excommunicated Roman Catholic
clergy, notably Father Dominic Kataribabo (who held an advanced theology degree from
the United States) and Father Joseph Kasapurari, the group formed an austere,
secluded communal settlement at Kanungu in the Rukungiri District.

The movement grew rapidly during the 1990s, drawing thousands of followers across
Uganda and neighboring Rwanda. Its growth was fueled by widespread social trauma, the
devastating regional impact of the HIV/AIDS epidemic, and apocalyptic anxieties
surrounding the upcoming year 2000. Members sold their land, livestock, and personal
possessions, turning all proceeds over to the movement's leadership. When the
prophesied apocalypse failed to occur on December 31, 1999, leadership recalibrated
the date of Mary's cleansing intervention to March 17, 2000.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

The MRTCG represented a radical, schismatic offshoot of post-Vatican II African
Catholicism. The leadership published a 160-page apocalyptic manifesto titled *A
Timely Message from Heaven: The End of the Present Times*, detailing visions in which
Jesus Christ and the Virgin Mary warned that the world would be engulfed in fire and
pestilence because humanity had broken the Ten Commandments. The Virgin Mary was
venerated as the supreme mediatrix whose intercession held back God's righteous anger.

Daily life in the Kanungu compound was characterized by extreme ascetic discipline.
To prevent members from violating the commandment against bearing false witness or
taking God's name in vain, the community instituted a comprehensive vow of silence,
requiring members to communicate almost entirely through sign language. Soap and sex
were strictly banned, meals were minimal, and members wore uniform robes—green for
novices, black for full members, and white for leadership. The movement functioned as
a total institution cut off from outside civic and religious authorities.

## Documented harms and controversies

On March 17, 2000, a massive explosion and fire incinerated the movement's church
compound in Kanungu, killing hundreds of congregants who had gathered for a
celebratory feast. The building's windows had been boarded shut and the doors locked
from the outside, with sulfuric acid and fuel accelerating the blaze.

Although initial media reports characterized the tragedy as a collective mass suicide,
forensic investigations by Ugandan authorities and international criminologists
unearthed a premeditated campaign of systematic mass murder. In the weeks following
the Kanungu fire, police uncovered multiple mass graves concealed beneath the floors
of movement properties across southwestern Uganda: 153 bodies at Buhunga, 155 at
Father Kataribabo's estate in Rugazi, 81 at Rushojwa, and 55 in a compound in Buziga
near Kampala. Victims showed evidence of lethal poisoning, strangulation, and
stabbing inflicted days and weeks prior to the final fire. The killings were
instigated by leadership to eliminate members who had demanded the return of their
assets after the millennial prophecy failed. With at least 778 documented fatalities,
the Kanungu massacre stands as one of the deadliest cult-related mass murder events in
modern history. Leaders Credonia Mwerinde and Joseph Kibwetere vanished and were never
apprehended.
