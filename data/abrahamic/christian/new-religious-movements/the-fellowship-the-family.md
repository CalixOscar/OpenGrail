# The Fellowship

## Historical context

The Fellowship (also known informally as "The Family" and legally incorporated under
entities including International Christian Leadership and the Fellowship Foundation)
was established in 1935 by Abraham Vereide (1886–1969), a Norwegian-born Methodist
evangelist. During the 1934 West Coast waterfront strike in Seattle, Vereide gathered
a circle of business executives and civic leaders for weekly prayer breakfasts to
counter what they perceived as communist labor agitation with Christian principles of
reconciliation and free enterprise.

Vereide moved to Washington, D.C., in the early 1940s, establishing prayer groups
within the United States Senate and House of Representatives. In 1953, with the
participation of President Dwight D. Eisenhower and Senator Frank Carlson, Vereide
inaugurated the annual Presidential Prayer Breakfast (later renamed the National
Prayer Breakfast), which has been attended by every sitting U.S. president since.
Following Vereide's death, leadership passed to Douglas Evans Coe (1928–2017), who led
the movement for nearly five decades. Under Coe, the Fellowship maintained an
intentionally discreet, low-profile posture, expanding its quiet diplomatic and
spiritual network into dozens of foreign parliaments and presidential palaces while
operating headquarters out of The Cedars, a historic estate in Arlington, Virginia.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

The Fellowship's theological philosophy is termed "Jesus plus nothing." Recommending
avoidance of traditional theological jargon, denominational labels, and religious
proselytizing, the movement presents Jesus of Nazareth not as the head of an
ecclesiastical institution, but as a revolutionary model of personal leadership and
unconditional friendship. Coe taught that power is ordained by God and that
believers are called to minister directly to the "key men" who wield political and
military authority—the "up and out" rather than the "down and out."

The movement operates through a decentralized network of small, private prayer cells
where political adversaries (such as Republicans and Democrats or warring foreign
diplomats) meet off the record to pray, confess personal struggles, and build
interpersonal trust. The Fellowship played documented informal backchannel roles in
major international diplomatic breakthroughs, including the 1978 Camp David Accords
and diplomatic reconciliations in Central America and the African Great Lakes region.
Scholarly analyses note the unique synthesis of evangelical piety, elite political
access, and informal international diplomacy that distinguishes The Fellowship from
overtly partisan religious advocacy groups.
