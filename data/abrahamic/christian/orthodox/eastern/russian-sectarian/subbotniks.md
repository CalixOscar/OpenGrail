# Subbotniks

## Historical context

The Subbotniks (*Subbotniki*, "Sabbatarians", from *Subbota*, Russian for
Saturday) appeared in the documentary record during the reign of Catherine the
Great in the 1770s among Russian peasants in the Voronezh, Tambov, and Saratov
provinces. Intensive reading of the Old Testament led groups of peasants to
repudiate Orthodox Christianity, the doctrine of the Trinity, the veneration of
icons and relics, and Sunday worship in favor of the biblical commandments of the
Torah.

Tsarist authorities viewed the spread of "Judaizing" among ethnic Russian
peasants with deep alarm, categorizing the Subbotniks as a dangerous religious
sect. Under Alexander I and Nicholas I, harsh imperial decrees subjected
Subbotniks to forced military conscription, denial of civil rights, and mass
expulsions to peripheral regions of the empire, particularly the North Caucasus,
Transcaucasia (such as the settlement of Privolnoye in Azerbaijan), and Siberia.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

The historical genealogy of the Subbotniks remains a subject of scholarly
discussion. Imperial officials and some early historians classified them as an
extreme offshoot of the Molokan movement (*Molokane-Subbotniki*). However,
modern scholarship emphasizes that while some Subbotnik groups did emerge from
disillusioned Molokan circles, others developed autonomously directly from
Orthodox peasant biblicism without prior Molokan affiliation.

The movement historically divided into two main liturgical and theological
branches:

1. **Non-Talmudic or Scriptural Subbotniks:** Adhered strictly to the literal
   commandments of the written Tanakh while rejecting rabbinic oral traditions,
   bearing parallels to Karaite Judaism.
2. **Talmudic Subbotniks (*Gery* / Proselytes):** Actively established ties with
   Ashkenazi Jewish communities in the Pale of Settlement, learned Hebrew, adopted
   the Talmud, the *Shulchan Aruch*, and traditional Jewish prayer books
   (*siddurim*), and underwent formal halakhic conversions.

In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, many Subbotnik families
migrated to Ottoman Palestine during the First and Second Aliyah, becoming
pioneers in early agricultural settlements in the Galilee (including Sejera /
Ilaniya and Kfar Tavor) and defending nascent communities (such as Alexander
Zaid and the Dubrovin family). Subbotnik descendants and active communities
exist today in Israel, Russia, Armenia, and across the former Soviet Union.
