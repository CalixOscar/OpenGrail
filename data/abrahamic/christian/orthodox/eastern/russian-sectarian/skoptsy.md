# Skoptsy

## Historical context

The Skoptsy movement emerged in the Oryol and Tula provinces of central Russia
during the early 1770s under the leadership of Kondraty Selivanov, a peasant
associated with the Khlyst conventicles. Selivanov broke with the Khlysts on the
grounds that their spiritual discipline was insufficient to eliminate sexual
temptation, asserting that true purity required the physical excision of the
sexual organs.

Arrested and exiled to Siberia under Catherine the Great in 1775, Selivanov was
later released during the reign of Emperor Paul I and returned to Saint
Petersburg. Under Emperor Alexander I, the movement experienced a period of
clandestine growth, attracting adherents across various social strata, including
merchants, civil servants, and military officers. Selivanov established a
residence at the Smolny hospice, where he presided over assemblies until his
confinement in 1820 to the Suzdal Monastery of Saint Euthymius, where he died in
1832.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Skoptsy theological doctrine interpreted the biblical Fall as the acquisition of
carnal sexual anatomy. Surgical removal was designated as the "fiery baptism" or
"seal" (*pechat*), derived from allegorical readings of the Gospels (such as
Matthew 19:12) and the Book of Revelation. The community distinguished two main
surgical procedures for men: the "lesser seal" (*malyi kreshchenie* or
orchiectomy), and the "greater seal" (*tsarskaya pechat* or total penectomy and
orchiectomy). Female adherents underwent surgical excisions of the breasts,
nipples, or labia.

In addition to physiological asceticism, Skoptsy mythography identified Selivanov
both as a redeemer figure and as Emperor Peter III, who was believed to have
escaped assassination to establish the true kingdom of God. Despite severe legal
proscription, loss of civil rights, and systematic mass exile to remote regions
such as the Yakutsk territory and the Caucasus throughout the nineteenth century,
the Skoptsy maintained highly organized mutual-aid networks and achieved
considerable commercial and financial success as money-changers, coachmen, and
grain merchants.

The movement declined sharply in the early twentieth century. Soviet authorities
subjected remaining Skoptsy communities to extensive criminal trials, anti-religious
suppression, and forced labor during the late 1920s and 1930s, after which the
tradition ceased to function as an organized religious body.
