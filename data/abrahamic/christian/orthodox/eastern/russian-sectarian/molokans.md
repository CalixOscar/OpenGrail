# Molokans

## Historical context

The Molokans (*Molokane*, "Milk Drinkers", a label initially applied by Orthodox
critics because they consumed dairy products during the Great Lent fasts, which
the group embraced citing 1 Peter 2:2, "desire the sincere milk of the word")
originated in the Tambov province during the 1760s. Their central founder, Semën
Uklein, was a tailor and son-in-law of Doukhobor leader Ilarion Pobirokhin.
Uklein broke decisively with the Doukhobors over their rejection of the written
Bible, arguing that divine truth was definitively preserved in the scriptural text
rather than solely in unwritten human inspiration.

Uklein and his followers presented formal confessions of faith to imperial
authorities, requesting freedom of worship and exemption from military service.
Subjected to severe repression, arrests, and forced resettlement, large numbers of
Molokans were banished during the nineteenth century to the Caucasus (Armenia,
Georgia, and Azerbaijan), the Crimea, and the Amur basin in Siberia, where they
established flourishing agricultural colonies.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Molokan doctrine represents a unique synthesis of radical biblicism and
Spiritual Christian interiority. While sharing the Protestant Reformation's
sola scriptura principle, Molokans reject physical water baptism and bread-and-wine
communion, interpreting all sacramental commands symbolically: baptism is the
reception of God's Word and the Holy Spirit, while communion is spiritual
fellowship and prayer. They reject the veneration of icons, the sign of the
cross, ecclesiastical vestments, and monasticism.

Molokan assemblies (*sobraniia*) are held in plain prayer houses or homes, led
by elected presbyters (*presvitery*), elders, and readers (*skazateli*). Worship
features unmetered, polyphonic chanting of biblical psalms, communal prayers on
knees, and shared meals. Molokans observe strict dietary prohibitions against
pork, predatory animals, and alcohol.

In the 1830s, the movement experienced a major internal division resulting in two
primary branches: the Constant Molokans (*Postoyannye*), who maintained traditional,
sober biblical services; and the Jumpers (*Pryguny*), led by charismatic prophets
such as Maksim Rudometkin, who incorporated ecstatic jumping, prophetic trances,
and foot-washing rituals. Facing compulsory military conscription in the early
twentieth century, thousands of Molokans emigrated from the Transcaucasus to the
United States (primarily California and Oregon), Mexico, and Australia. The
tradition remains a living community in both the diaspora and the post-Soviet
republics.
