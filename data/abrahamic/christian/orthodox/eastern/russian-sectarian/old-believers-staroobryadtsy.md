# Old Believers

## Historical context

The Raskol (Schism) of the Russian Orthodox Church erupted in the mid-seventeenth
century when Patriarch Nikon introduced sweeping liturgical and textual reforms
(1652–1666) designed to conform Russian practice to contemporary Greek Orthodox
usage. Traditionalist clergy and laity, prominently championed by Archpriest
Avvakum Petrov, vehemently rejected the changes—including making the sign of
the cross with three fingers rather than two, spelling Jesus as *Iisus* rather
than *Isus*, singing a triple alleluia, and adopting newly corrected Greek
service books. Traditionalists viewed these revisions as apostasy that defiled
the holy customs of ancient Rus'.

The Church Council of 1666–1667 formally endorsed the Nikonian reforms and
anathematized all who maintained the old rites, triggering severe imperial and
ecclesiastical persecution. Thousands of Old Believers were imprisoned, executed,
or driven into mass self-immolation in defense of their faith, while large
populations fled to remote borderlands of the empire, including the Russian
North, the Urals, Siberia, and the Don region.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Following the schism and the natural death of sympathetic pre-reform bishops,
Old Believers split over the theology of the priesthood into two major branches:
the *Popovtsy* (priestists) and the *Bezpopovtsy* (priestless). The Popovtsy
maintained the necessity of all seven sacraments and the historic episcopate,
eventually establishing their own hierarchies in the nineteenth and twentieth
centuries (most notably the Belokrinitskaya hierarchy in 1846 and the Russian
Old-Orthodox Church). The Bezpopovtsy, by contrast, concluded that the
episcopate and true priesthood had vanished from the earth under the reign of
the Antichrist, restricting valid sacramental life to baptism and lay confession
while dividing into numerous conciliar sub-confessions (such as the Pomortsy and
Fedoseevtsy).

Old Believers developed tightly knit, self-reliant agrarian and merchant
communities known for strict moral discipline, total abstinence from alcohol and
tobacco, high literacy, and the preservation of pre-Petrine cultural and
musical traditions (including Znamenny chant). Surviving severe Soviet repression
and collectivization, Old Believers remain a living, globally dispersed communion
with active communities across Russia, Eastern Europe, North America, and South
America.
