# Doukhobors

## Historical context

The Doukhobors (*Dukhobortsy*, "Spirit Wrestlers") emerged in the mid-eighteenth
century among Russian peasants in the Voronezh, Tambov, and Yekaterinoslav
provinces. The name was coined in 1785 by Archbishop Ambrosius of Yekaterinoslav
as an accusation that the sect wrestled against the Holy Spirit; the community
adopted the title, reinterpreting it as wrestling *for* and *alongside* the
Spirit against worldly corruption. Under early leaders such as Siluan Kolesnikov,
Ilarion Pobirokhin, and Savely Kapustin, the Doukhobors rejected the authority of
the Russian Orthodox Church and the Tsarist state.

Tolerated under Alexander I and resettled along the Molochna River in southern
Ukraine, the Doukhobors developed a prosperous communal economy before being
forcibly exiled to the Transcaucasus by Nicholas I in the 1840s. In 1895, under the
leadership of Petr V. Verigin, the movement staged a coordinated public burning
of their weapons across several Caucasian settlements (the "Burning of the Arms")
to protest compulsory military service, prompting severe state brutality. With the
assistance of author Leo Tolstoy, British and American Quakers, and Canadian
authorities, approximately 7,500 Doukhobors emigrated to western Canada in 1899,
establishing agricultural settlements in Saskatchewan and later British Columbia.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Doukhobor theology is anti-sacerdotal and radically immanentist. God is not
conceived as a distant entity or trinity, but as the spirit of love, truth, and
reason dwelling within every human consciousness. Consequently, killing any human
being constitutes the destruction of the divine temple. Doukhobors reject
material icons, church buildings, baptism by water, and the Eucharist,
interpreting sacraments metaphorically as inner spiritual transformations.

Unlike scriptural biblicists such as the Molokans, Doukhobors historically
subordinated the written Bible to their oral tradition, known as the *Book of
Life* (*Zhivotnaia Kniga*), comprising memorized psalms, hymns, and moral maxims
chanted antiphonally in communal worship. In Canada, tensions with government
authorities over homestead land patents, mandatory public schooling, and vital
statistics registration generated divisions into three main branches: the
Communal Doukhobors (Union of Spiritual Communities of Christ), the Independent
Doukhobors, and the radical Sons of Freedom (*Svobodniki*). Doukhobor culture
persists as a living religious tradition in Canada, Russia, and the Caucasus.
