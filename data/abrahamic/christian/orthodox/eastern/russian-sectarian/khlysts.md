# Khlysts

## Historical context

The Khlysts (self-designated as *Khristovshchina*, "Christ-faith", or *Lyudi
Bozhii*, "People of God"; the name *Khlysty*, meaning flagellants, was a
derogatory pun applied by Orthodox polemicists) arose in central Russia in the
mid-seventeenth century. Tradition traces the movement to a peasant or soldier
named Danila Filippovich in the Kostroma region, who was said to have descended
from heaven on Mount Gorodina in the person of God the Father (Sabaoth), issuing
twelve moral commandments. He designated Ivan Suslov as his spiritual son and
living Christ.

Khlysts organized themselves into autonomous secret conventicles called *korabli*
("ships"), each governed by a spiritual father ("Christ" or "pilot") and a
spiritual mother ("Mother of God" or "prophetess"). Members maintained outward
participation in the Russian Orthodox Church—attending services, taking communion,
and having their children baptized—while regarding these external rituals as
purely formal concessions, reserving their true spiritual allegiance for secret
nocturnal assemblies.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

The focal point of Khlyst religious life was the *radenie* ("spiritual striving"
or "zeal"), held in private homes or secluded forest huts. Dressed in white linen
robes, participants sang spiritual verses (*dukhovnye stikhi*), accelerated
rhythmic movement, and engaged in circular running and spinning dances that
induced hyperventilation, exhaustion, and ecstatic trance states believed to
culminate in the descent of the Holy Spirit and spontaneous prophecy.

Although hostile church and state investigators routinely accused the sect of
nocturnal orgies and incestuous promiscuity, scholarly analysis reveals that
authentic Khlyst theology demanded strict asceticism. Members were forbidden from
consuming alcohol, smoking tobacco, using profanity, and engaging in carnal
relations, with married converts urged to live as brother and sister. The Khlyst
milieu gave birth to several offshoot movements, most directly the Skoptsy in the
late eighteenth century, before gradually fading in the early twentieth century
under intensive Tsarist police scrutiny and subsequent Soviet antireligious
campaigns.
