# Polish Autocephalous Orthodox Church

## Historical formation

After an independent Polish state reappeared following the First World War, Orthodox dioceses formerly linked to the Russian church sought a new canonical settlement. Constantinople granted autocephaly in 1924, while Moscow rejected that act and issued its own recognition in 1948. War, border changes, forced population transfers, and communist governance greatly altered the church’s demographic base.

## Identity and practice

The metropolitan of Warsaw presides over a synodal church that worships mainly in Church Slavonic and Polish, with Belarusian, Ukrainian, and other local identities represented among its faithful. It is part of Eastern Orthodox communion and not a nationally separate creed. Its two twentieth-century recognitions also illustrate how autocephaly can involve contested canonical and political histories.
