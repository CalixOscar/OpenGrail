# Martinism & Ordre des Élus Coëns

## Historical context

Martinism traces its lineage to two distinct eighteenth-century French esoteric figures: the theurgist Jacques de Livron Joachim de la Tour de la Casa Martinez de Pasqually (c. 1727–1774) and his student, the philosopher Louis-Claude de Saint-Martin (1743–1803, known as *Le Philosophe Inconnu*). In 1767, Pasqually founded the *Ordre des Chevaliers Maçons Élus Coëns de l'Univers* (Order of Knight-Masons Elect Priests of the Universe) in Bordeaux, establishing chapters in Paris, Lyon, and Versailles. The Élus Coëns operated as an elite esoteric high-degree fraternity superposed onto Freemasonry, practicing complex operative theurgical ceremonies aimed at contacting celestial intermediaries.

Following Pasqually's death in Saint-Domingue in 1774, two major developments occurred. Jean-Baptiste Willermoz (1730–1824) integrated Pasqually's esoteric doctrine into high-degree Freemasonry, creating the Rectified Scottish Rite (*Chevaliers Bienfaisants de la Cité Sainte*). Concurrently, Louis-Claude de Saint-Martin moved away from operative theurgy and ritual magic toward an interior, introspective mysticism heavily influenced by the writings of Jakob Böhme, known as the "Way of the Heart" (*la Voie Cardiaque*). Saint-Martin transmitted this initiatory current through personal disciple lines without formal lodge fees or rigid governance.

In 1888–1891, the French occultists Gérard Encausse ("Papus") and Augustin Chaboseau revived and formalized the tradition into the *Ordre Martiniste* in Paris, establishing a formal Supreme Council and a three-degree initiatory structure culminating in the grade of *Supérieur Inconnu* (S:::I:::). The Martinist Order expanded internationally into Russia, the Americas, and Western Europe, and continues to operate globally through various traditional, autonomous Martinist jurisdictions and fraternities.

## Distinctives and comparative notes

Martinism is distinguished within Western esotericism by its explicit Christian theosophic foundation. Unlike deistic Freemasonry or secularized occult currents, Martinist cosmology is centered upon Christ as the Great Architect of the Universe and the Divine Reparator (*le Réparateur*). Its soteriology is set forth in Pasqually's *Treatise on the Reintegration of Beings*, which posits that humanity was created as a divine, luminous, androgynous guardian of creation. Following the metaphysical fall into gross material embodiment, human souls must undergo spiritual regeneration and active spiritual purification to regain their primal divine stature.

While the original eighteenth-century Élus Coëns utilized ceremonial magic, protective circles, and fasting to evoke sensible luminous spiritual manifestations (*la Chose*), mainstream modern Martinism follows Saint-Martin's inward, contemplative path. In this interior approach, the individual's heart is viewed as the true temple where the divine fire is rekindled through moral refinement, charity, and devotional meditation.
